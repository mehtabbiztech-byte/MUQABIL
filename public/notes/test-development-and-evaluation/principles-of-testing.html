Principles of Testing

Every defensible test can be audited against the same short list of principles — purpose, alignment, fairness, consistency and practicality

  • ⏱️ 16 min read
  • 📚 8 sections
  • 🔑 12 key terms
  • 🧩 3 worked examples
  • 📝 10 MCQs · 4 CRQs · 1 ERQ

The big idea

Principles of testing are the standards a test must satisfy if its results are to support decisions. They are not abstract virtues: each one has a practical indicator you can check on a real paper. The core set is purpose (the test answers a stated question for a named user), validity (it measures the intended outcomes), reliability (results are consistent), fairness (no learner is advantaged or disadvantaged by irrelevant factors), objectivity (scoring is independent of the marker’s mood), comprehensiveness (it samples the domain, not a convenient corner), practicality (it can be built, administered, marked and reported within available resources) and ethics (it is secure, confidential and does not harm learners). A test that fails any one of these can still produce numbers; it cannot produce defensible decisions.

What you will master

  1. State the principles of testing and give a concrete indicator for each that can be checked on a real paper.
  2. Audit a test against the principles and identify which failures matter most for a stated decision.
  3. Resolve the tension between validity, fairness and practicality when resources are limited.

Foundations

Core concepts

Six ideas carry this topic. Each card gives the definition, the wording examiners use, and what the idea looks like in a real test.

6 cards
🎯 01 / 06

Purpose: every test answers a question

A test must have a stated purpose and a named user — learner, teacher, parent, institution or system — because these determine content, difficulty, format and reporting. “To see how they are doing” is not a purpose. A purpose statement identifies the decision the result will support, which in turn dictates the evidence required.

Exam wording“The purpose of a test determines its content, its form and the kind of score it reports.”

In practiceWrite the purpose sentence before writing the first item: “This test will help me decide which learners need reteaching on X.”
🧭 02 / 06

Validity and alignment

Validity is the extent to which a test measures what it claims, for the purpose intended. At classroom level this is mainly content validity — representative coverage of the taught outcomes at the right cognitive level, achieved through a table of specifications. A test that is well marked but assesses the wrong outcomes is precisely and uselessly measured.

Exam wording“Validity is the degree to which a test measures what it is intended to measure.”

In practiceCheck every item against an outcome; delete any item that cannot be traced to one.
🔁 03 / 06

Reliability, objectivity and consistency

Reliability is consistency of results across occasions, forms and markers; objectivity is the narrower requirement that scoring does not depend on who marks. Both are served by clear items, adequate length, a marking scheme, rubrics, anchor scripts and moderation. Reliability is necessary for validity but not sufficient: a consistently wrong measurement is still wrong.

Exam wording“Reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity.”

In practiceSecond-mark ten scripts from another section and compare; disagreement is your reliability data.
⚖️ 04 / 06

Fairness, bias and access

Fairness requires that the test measures the intended construct and not irrelevant factors: language complexity, cultural references, gender or rural stereotypes, cost of materials, or speed when speed is not the construct. Bias is systematic, not random. Access arrangements remove barriers — extra time, a reader, larger print — without changing the standard.

Exam wording“A test is fair when no learner is advantaged or disadvantaged by factors irrelevant to the construct being measured.”

In practiceRead every item aloud to yourself: if the reading demand exceeds the subject demand, rewrite it.
📐 05 / 06

Comprehensiveness and discrimination

Comprehensiveness means the test samples the whole domain in proportion to its importance, not only the topics that are easy to set. Discrimination is the capacity of the test to distinguish learners who have mastered the outcomes from those who have not — which requires a spread of difficulty and items that test more than recall.

Exam wording“A comprehensive test samples all intended outcomes in proportion to their emphasis in the curriculum.”

In practiceDraw the topic × level grid before writing items; if one cell is empty, the paper is unbalanced.
🧰 06 / 06

Practicality and ethics

Practicality covers time, cost, materials, marking load and reporting: an excellent test that cannot be marked in time produces no usable evidence. Ethics covers security, confidentiality, informed expectations and the avoidance of harm — including the anxiety created by unnecessary high-stakes testing and the misuse of results to label learners.

Exam wording“Practicality concerns whether a test can be constructed, administered, scored and interpreted with the resources available.”

In practiceEstimate marking time before you set the paper; if it exceeds the time available, shorten the constructed-response section.

04

Application

Worked examples

Three walkthroughs take the definitions to the level the paper asks for: a calculation, a judgement and a repair.

3 walkthroughs
Audit

Auditing a class test against the principles

A 40-mark Grade 8 science test contains 32 recall items and 8 marks of extended response. It was written the night before, marked out of 40 without a scheme, and returned three weeks later. Two items use vocabulary above the class reading level.

  1. Purpose: unstated. There is no evidence about which decision the test supports, so the remaining judgements lack a reference point.
  2. Validity: recall items dominate a paper that should sample application, so content validity is weak against the practical outcomes of the curriculum.
  3. Reliability and objectivity: no scheme and no moderation, so marks depend on the marker’s judgement; a three-week return makes the evidence useless formatively.
  4. Fairness: two items test reading, not science, for learners working in a second language.
  5. Practicality: it was quick to set but expensive in re-teaching time, because the evidence arrived too late to change anything.
Result Four of eight principles fail. The cheapest fixes are a blueprint (validity), a marking scheme (reliability) and a return within one week (practicality and washback).
Why this works

Auditing in this order prevents the common mistake of fixing what is easy — neatening the paper — while leaving the structural failures untouched. Purpose comes first because it defines what validity means for this test.

Repair

Rewriting a biased item

An item reads: “A cricket team scores 240 runs in 40 overs. Calculate the run rate. (2 marks)” A teacher notices that girls in the class, who play hockey, consistently skip it.

  1. Diagnose the construct: the target is rate calculation, not knowledge of cricket.
  2. Identify the irrelevant demand: the cricket context adds a comprehension step and a cultural association that is not part of the construct.
  3. Neutralise the context: “A water tank fills 240 litres in 40 minutes. Calculate the rate in litres per minute.”
  4. Check the rest of the paper: look for other items where context, names, places or units create the same advantage.
Result The repaired item tests the same operation with no irrelevant context; a context review across the paper prevents the same bias elsewhere.
Why this works

Fairness is not achieved by removing all context — context can support understanding. It is achieved by ensuring the context does not add a demand that is unrelated to the construct and unevenly distributed across groups.

Trade-off

Balancing validity and practicality

A teacher has 45 learners, one 40-minute lesson for assessment and two hours for marking. The outcome is “design a fair test and justify the variables”.

  1. Recognise the constraint: a full investigation is the most valid task but cannot be run, observed and marked within the budget.
  2. Preserve the construct: keep the design decision, which is the core of the outcome, and remove the execution.
  3. Choose a compressed format: a one-page design brief — variables table, controlled variables, one paragraph of justification — marked with a three-criterion rubric.
  4. Sample for reliability: moderate six scripts with a colleague and adjust the rubric descriptors before marking the rest.
Result A 25-minute design brief with a three-criterion rubric assesses the same decision-making at a fraction of the cost, with moderation protecting consistency.
Why this works

Practicality failures are usually solved by narrowing the evidence rather than lowering the standard. Keeping the cognitive demand and shrinking the artefact preserves validity while making the assessment feasible.

Practice

Classroom and test-room application

The left column is the professional standard; the right column lists the errors that cost marks and damage results.

do / avoid

✅ Do this

  • Write a purpose statement and name the user of the result before designing the test.
  • Trace every item to an outcome and build the paper from a table of specifications.
  • Write the marking scheme or rubric before administering the test.
  • Check reading demand, cultural references and access needs for fairness.
  • Estimate marking and reporting time before committing to the format.
  • Moderate a sample of scripts and store papers securely; treat results as confidential.

🚫 Avoid this

  • Do not set a test because it is the end of term; set it because a decision needs evidence.
  • Do not accept a test as valid merely because it is reliable.
  • Do not let one section of a grade sit a harder or differently marked paper without moderation.
  • Do not use complex language or unfamiliar contexts that add demands unrelated to the construct.
  • Do not publish ranks or results in ways that label learners publicly.
  • Do not design a paper you cannot mark and return in time for the evidence to be useful.

Exam traps

Misconceptions

Each myth is a plausible sentence that loses marks. Learn the reality column as the correction you can write.

4 corrections
MythA reliable test is automatically a valid one.
RealityReliability is consistency; validity is measuring the right thing. A test can be consistently measuring the wrong outcome — precise and useless.
MythFairness means every learner gets exactly the same paper and conditions.
RealityFairness means the same construct is assessed. Approved access arrangements change the route, not the standard, so identical treatment can be unfair.
MythComprehensive coverage means asking at least one question on every topic.
RealityCoverage must be proportionate to emphasis and cognitive level, not merely present. One recall item per topic can still leave the paper unbalanced.
MythIf a test is valid and reliable, practicality does not matter.
RealityAn unmarkable test produces no usable evidence. Practicality is a genuine principle because assessment only works if it can be completed, scored and returned in time to inform teaching.

Exam room

Exam-style questions

Answer first, then open the model. The chips show the command word, the mark value and the cognitive level being tested.

2 questions
Discuss 10 marks Evaluate

“A test that is valid and reliable is a good test.” Discuss this statement with reference to at least four principles of testing.

Model answer

The statement is only partly true. Validity and reliability are central: a test that does not measure the intended outcomes, or that yields inconsistent results, cannot support defensible decisions. But a good test must also satisfy other principles. Fairness requires that no learner is advantaged or disadvantaged by factors irrelevant to the construct, such as unnecessary reading demand or culturally specific contexts; a valid and reliable test can still be biased. Comprehensiveness requires proportionate coverage of the domain rather than a convenient corner, so a test may be valid for the topics it includes yet misrepresent the curriculum. Practicality requires that the test can be built, administered, marked and reported within available resources, because an unmarkable or late-returned paper produces no usable evidence and no feedback. Finally, ethics and purpose matter: a technically sound test used for a decision it was not designed to support, or published in a way that labels learners, is harmful. The statement is therefore necessary but not sufficient; a good test satisfies all the principles in proportion to the stakes of the decision.

Apply 8 marks Apply

A Grade 9 teacher must assess ‘evaluate the reliability of a news report’ with 45 learners, one lesson and limited marking time. Explain how the principles of validity and practicality can both be respected.

Model answer

The construct is evaluation, so the task must require a judgement with justification rather than recall of a definition. To respect practicality, the evidence can be compressed: give two short contrasting news extracts on one page and ask for a structured evaluation — two credibility criteria applied to each extract, and a justified overall judgement — in 25 minutes. This keeps the cognitive demand, which is where validity lives, while controlling the artefact. A three-criterion analytic rubric (criteria applied, evidence cited, judgement justified) with level descriptors allows consistent marking, and moderating a sample of six scripts before marking the rest protects reliability and therefore the defensibility of the results. Access needs such as extra time should be planned in advance for fairness. The lesson length and marking budget are respected, and the results can be returned within a week so that the evidence still informs teaching.

Compress

Takeaways and revision

Revise these six lines the night before the paper, then tick the checklist once each one is retrievable without notes.

6 lines

Six things to remember

  1. A test is defensible only when its purpose and the user of its results are stated.
  2. Validity is alignment with the intended outcomes, chiefly through a table of specifications.
  3. Reliability and objectivity come from clear items, marking schemes, rubrics and moderation.
  4. Fairness means the construct is assessed, not reading speed, cultural familiarity or access to materials.
  5. Comprehensiveness is proportionate coverage of the domain and its cognitive levels.
  6. Practicality and ethics decide whether a technically sound test can actually be used well.
One-line summary

The principles of testing — purpose, validity, reliability, fairness, objectivity, comprehensiveness, practicality and ethics — are a checklist for defensibility, and every one of them has a concrete indicator you can inspect on a real paper.

💡
Exam tip

Questions that ask you to “evaluate a test” expect the principles as headings. Name the principle, give the indicator from the paper, then state the consequence — that structure earns marks faster than a paragraph of opinion.
