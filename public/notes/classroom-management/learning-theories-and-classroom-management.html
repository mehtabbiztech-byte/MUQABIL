Learning Theories and Classroom Management

Five psychological lenses that explain why learners behave as they do — and how to manage a classroom that teaches

⏱️ 10 min read 📚 7 sections 🔑 12 key terms 📝 10 MCQs · 4 CRQs · 1 ERQ

The big idea

Classroom management is not a bag of tricks; it is applied psychology. Behaviorism explains how consequences shape routines, cognitivism explains attention and cognitive load, constructivism explains cooperative norms and shared responsibility, humanism explains belonging and motivation, and social cognitive theory explains modelling and self-regulation. Effective teachers read a situation through several theories at once and choose the least intrusive response that still teaches the desired behaviour.

What you will master

  1. Explain how behaviorism, cognitivism, constructivism, humanism and social cognitive theory each account for classroom behaviour.
  2. Match a management response to the theory that justifies it, and justify why it is the least intrusive option.
  3. Apply Kounin, Dreikurs, Glasser and Rogers to a real classroom incident.

Foundations

Core concepts

Each card isolates one idea examiners test. Read the definition first, then the In practice line — that is where scenario questions begin.

🔔 01

Behaviorism (Pavlov, Watson, Thorndike, Skinner)

Behaviour is shaped by antecedents and consequences: reinforcement increases behaviour, punishment suppresses it, extinction removes it, and shaping builds complex routines from small steps. Rules, routines, prompts, praise, token systems and behaviour contracts all rest on behaviourist logic.

In practice

State the expected behaviour, model it, reinforce it immediately and consistently, then thin the reinforcement as the routine becomes automatic.

💾 02

Cognitivism (Piaget, Ausubel, Gagné, Sweller)

Attention, working memory and schema drive behaviour. Overloaded learners fidget, withdraw or disrupt; unclear task structures produce off-task behaviour. Advance organisers, chunked instructions, worked examples and predictable lesson architecture reduce the load and therefore the misbehaviour.

In practice

Give one instruction at a time, keep a visible agenda on the board, and use worked examples before independent practice.

🏗️ 03

Constructivism (Piaget, Vygotsky, Bruner)

Learners co-construct norms and knowledge through interaction. Vygotsky’s ZPD and scaffolding imply that the teacher manages the social system — pairing, group roles, accountable talk — so learners can regulate themselves inside a community of practice.

In practice

Co-construct class agreements, assign rotating group roles, and scaffold group talk with sentence frames rather than policing noise.

💖 04

Humanism (Maslow, Rogers, Glasser)

Behaviour is an attempt to meet needs — safety, belonging, power, freedom, fun (Glasser) or physiological through self-actualisation (Maslow). Rogers adds unconditional positive regard. Most “defiance” is a need signal, so the first management question is “what need is this behaviour meeting?”

In practice

Meet the need behind the behaviour: give voice and responsibility, offer choice within limits, and keep the relationship unconditional even when correcting.

🌉 05

Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura)

Learners acquire behaviour by observing models and are regulated by self-efficacy, outcome expectations and self-regulation. Teacher calm, tone and consistency are the strongest models in the room; peer models are powerful for routines and for help-seeking.

In practice

Narrate your own regulation (“I’m going to take a breath and check the steps”), and let a peer model the routine correctly rather than correcting publicly.

⚙️ 06

Management theorists as applications of theory

Kounin supplies withitness, overlapping, smoothness and momentum; Ginott supplies congruent communication; Dreikurs supplies logical consequences and the four goals of misbehaviour (attention, power, revenge, inadequacy); Canter supplies assertive expectations; Jones supplies proximity and body language.

In practice

Diagnose the goal of the behaviour first, then match the response: attention → planned ignoring plus proximity; power → limited choice; inadequacy → scaffolded success.

02

Precision

Key vocabulary

Be able to define each term in one sentence. Bold terms below also appear highlighted inside the notes, so you can meet them in context.

Behaviorism
The school of psychology that defines learning as a change in observable behaviour produced by environmental stimuli, reinforcement and punishment (Pavlov, Watson, Thorndike, Skinner).
Operant conditioning
Learning in which the consequences that follow a behaviour change the likelihood of that behaviour recurring (Skinner).
Reinforcement
Any consequence that increases the frequency of the behaviour it follows. Positive reinforcement adds a stimulus; negative reinforcement removes an aversive one.
Shaping
Reinforcing successive approximations of a target behaviour until the complete behaviour is established.
Cognitive load
The amount of information working memory must process at once. Overload shows up as fidgeting, withdrawal or off-task behaviour.
Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)
Vygotsky’s term for the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with support.
Scaffolding
Temporary, graduated support that is withdrawn as competence grows.
Withitness
Kounin’s term for a teacher’s awareness of everything happening in the room, noticing problems before they spread.
Overlapping
Kounin’s term for managing two classroom events at once without abandoning the first.
Unconditional positive regard
Rogers’ stance of accepting the learner as a person of worth regardless of their behaviour.
Self-efficacy
Bandura’s term for a learner’s belief in their own capability to succeed at a specific task.
Logical consequences
Dreikurs’ principle that consequences must be related to the behaviour, respectful of the learner and reasonable in size.

04

Practice

Classroom application

What to do, and what to avoid, in a real classroom. The left column is the standard to teach; the right column lists the errors that create management problems.

✅ Do this

  • Diagnose before reacting: is this a skill gap, a needs gap or a structure gap?
  • Teach routines the way you teach content — model, rehearse, give feedback.
  • Reinforce the behaviour you want far more often than you punish the behaviour you don’t.
  • Use the least intrusive intervention that will work (proximity, eye contact, name + instruction).
  • Keep instructions short, sequenced and visible so working memory is free for learning.
  • Protect the relationship: correct the behaviour, never the person, and repair quickly after conflict.

🚫 Avoid this

  • Do not punish the whole class for the actions of a few — it damages belonging and creates resentment.
  • Do not rely on punitive consequences alone; punishment suppresses behaviour without teaching a replacement.
  • Do not escalate publicly — public confrontation triggers the power goal and forces a fight you cannot win.
  • Do not assume defiance: check hearing, language, vision, hunger, trauma or a learning difficulty first.
  • Do not use “because I said so” as your reasoning — explain the purpose of the routine.
  • Do not withdraw support as a sanction for a learner who needs it to access the task.
05

Exam traps

Common misconceptions

Each misconception below is a plausible-sounding statement that loses marks. Learn the reality column as a correction you can write.

Myth

“Good classroom management means a silent classroom.”

✔ Reality

Order is a means, not an end. Productive classrooms have purposeful talk, movement and collaboration; silence is appropriate only for specific tasks such as independent writing or assessment.

Myth

“Punishment teaches students how to behave.”

✔ Reality

Punishment only signals what not to do. Lasting behaviour change comes from teaching and reinforcing the replacement behaviour, which is why behaviourist shaping pairs a consequence with an alternative.

Myth

“Humanistic management is soft and permissive.”

✔ Reality

Humanistic management is demanding but respectful: high expectations, clear limits, choice within structure, and unconditional regard for the person. It is the opposite of neglect.

Myth

“One theory should drive all my decisions.”

✔ Reality

Different situations need different lenses: reinforcement for routines, cognitive load theory for instructions, constructivist structures for group work, and humanistic responses for conflict.

06

Compress

Key takeaways

Revise these lines the night before the paper — they compress the whole note into recall-ready statements.

Six things to remember

  1. Behaviourism supplies the mechanics of routines and consequences; it is strongest for procedures, habits and foundational skills.
  2. Cognitivism explains why unclear or overloaded tasks produce off-task behaviour — clarity is a management strategy.
  3. Constructivism makes norm-setting and group work a shared responsibility rather than a teacher imposition.
  4. Humanism reframes misbehaviour as an unmet need and prioritises belonging, voice and choice.
  5. Bandura’s social cognitive theory makes the teacher’s own regulated behaviour the most powerful management tool in the room.
  6. Kounin’s withitness, overlapping, smoothness and momentum remain the most practical bridge between theory and daily management.
One-line summary

Every classroom management decision is applied learning theory — diagnose the cause, choose the least intrusive response, and teach the behaviour you want rather than only punishing the behaviour you don’t.

💡
Exam tip

If a question asks “which theory best explains…”, name the theory, state its mechanism in one sentence, then give the classroom action. Marks are lost by describing the theory without applying it.
