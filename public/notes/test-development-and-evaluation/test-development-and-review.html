Test Development and Review

A test is built in stages and judged by its items — plan it, blueprint it, review it, pilot it, then let the data decide

  • ⏱️ 16 min read
  • 📚 8 sections
  • 🔑 12 key terms
  • 🧩 3 worked examples
  • 📝 10 MCQs · 4 CRQs · 1 ERQ

The big idea

Test development is a sequence of defensible decisions, not a single act of writing questions. It runs from a stated purpose and construct, through a table of specifications, item writing and pre-test review, to piloting, item analysis, assembly, administration, scoring and reporting — and then back to revision. Test review happens at two points: before administration, where colleagues check clarity, fairness and alignment, and after administration, where difficulty and discrimination indices show which items worked and which must be rewritten. In most Pakistani classrooms the sequence is compressed into an evening, but the logic is not optional: without a blueprint the paper samples whatever was easy to set, and without item analysis the same faulty items are reused for years.

What you will master

  1. Sequence the stages of test development and state the output and quality check of each.
  2. Construct a table of specifications that allocates marks across content and cognitive levels.
  3. Use difficulty and discrimination indices and distractor analysis to decide which items to keep, revise or discard.

Foundations

Core concepts

Six ideas carry this topic. Each card gives the definition, the wording examiners use, and what the idea looks like in a real test.

6 cards
📋 01 / 06

Stage 1 — Planning: purpose, construct and constraints

Development begins with a written test plan: the purpose and the decision the result will support, the construct and outcomes to be sampled, the learners and their level, the time and marks available, the item formats, the marking capacity and the reporting requirements. This plan is the reference for every later decision, and it is the document that makes review possible.

Exam wording“Test planning specifies the purpose, the content domain, the item formats and the practical constraints before items are written.”

In practiceWrite the plan on one page before you write a single item; most faulty papers are faulty at this stage.
🗺️ 02 / 06

Stage 2 — Blueprint: the table of specifications

The table of specifications (test blueprint) crosses content areas with cognitive levels and allocates marks or item numbers to each cell in proportion to teaching emphasis and curriculum weight. It is the operational definition of content validity: it prevents a paper from being dominated by the topics that are easiest to set and makes coverage auditable.

Exam wording“A table of specifications ensures that a test samples content areas and cognitive levels in the intended proportions.”

In practiceFill the grid with marks first, then write the number and type of items each cell needs.
✍️ 03 / 06

Stage 3 — Item writing and pre-test review

Items are written to the blueprint, using the format that fits each cell: selected-response items for broad coverage and constructed-response items for explanation and extended reasoning. Pre-test review has three passes — editorial (clarity, grammar, one idea per item), technical (single defensible key, plausible distractors, correct mark allocation) and sensitivity (bias, cultural assumptions, accessibility, unnecessary reading load). A colleague's review catches what the writer cannot see.

Exam wording“Items should be reviewed for clarity, technical quality and freedom from bias before administration.”

In practiceAsk a colleague to answer each item without the key; any disagreement is a defect to fix.
🧪 04 / 06

Stage 4 — Piloting and item analysis

Piloting administers the items to a comparable group to gather data before the marks count. Item analysis then computes the difficulty index (proportion correct) and the discrimination index (success of the top group minus the bottom group), and examines the distribution of choices for each distractor. These figures convert item writing from opinion into evidence.

Exam wording“Item analysis provides information about item difficulty and discrimination, which is used to revise or select items.”

In practiceAfter every class test, analyse five items; you will find at least one that needs rewriting.
🧾 05 / 06

Stage 5 — Assembly, administration and scoring

Assembly orders items by format and difficulty, writes clear instructions, fixes the time allowance and checks the mark total against the blueprint. Administration standardises conditions and access arrangements. Scoring requires a marking scheme with acceptable alternatives and partial credit rules, and moderation of a sample so that marks mean the same thing across markers and sections.

Exam wording“A marking scheme and moderation are essential for consistent scoring of constructed responses.”

In practiceWrite the marking scheme while writing the items — it exposes ambiguity immediately.
🔁 06 / 06

Stage 6 — Post-test review and the item bank

After administration the paper itself is reviewed: reliability estimates, item statistics, distractor performance, marking discrepancies and feedback from learners and teachers. Good items are stored in an item bank with their statistics and the outcome they assess; faulty items are revised or retired. This closes the loop and builds a departmental resource that improves every year instead of reproducing the same paper.

Exam wording“Post-test review uses item statistics and marking evidence to revise the test and improve future versions.”

In practiceRecord each item's difficulty, discrimination and outcome in a shared sheet; reuse only items that earned their place.

04

Application

Worked examples

Three walkthroughs take the definitions to the level the paper asks for: a calculation, a judgement and a repair.

3 walkthroughs
Blueprint

Building a 50-mark blueprint

A unit has three topics (A, B, C) taught over five weeks, with A and B emphasised more heavily than C. The paper must sample recall, application and analysis.

  1. Weight by teaching time and curriculum emphasis: A 40 per cent, B 40 per cent, C 20 per cent — that is 20, 20 and 10 of the 50 marks.
  2. Decide the level mix: 20 marks recall and understanding, 22 marks application, 8 marks analysis — roughly 40/44/16, which matches the unit’s stated outcomes.
  3. Fill the grid: allocate each cell’s marks, then choose the format — selected-response for recall and broad coverage, structured questions for application, one extended item for analysis.
  4. Convert to items and check totals: each cell gets a number of items whose marks add to its allocation, and the grand total is verified against 50 before writing.
Result A 3 × 3 grid totalling 50 marks: A 20, B 20, C 10 across recall/application/analysis, with formats chosen per cell.
Why this works

The blueprint converts vague intentions (“cover the unit”) into a checkable specification. Writing items to a filled grid prevents both over-testing favourite topics and an all-recall paper.

Item analysis

Deciding what to do with two items

Item 7: 28 of 40 learners answered correctly; in the top ten, 9 were correct; in the bottom ten, 3 were correct. Item 12: 30 of 40 correct; top ten 6 correct; bottom ten 7 correct.

  1. Item 7 difficulty: 28 ÷ 40 = 0.70 — a moderately easy item, which is appropriate for a core objective.
  2. Item 7 discrimination: (9 ÷ 10) − (3 ÷ 10) = 0.90 − 0.30 = 0.60 — strong positive discrimination.
  3. Item 12 difficulty: 30 ÷ 40 = 0.75 — also moderately easy.
  4. Item 12 discrimination: (6 ÷ 10) − (7 ÷ 10) = 0.60 − 0.70 = −0.10 — negative, so weaker learners outperformed stronger ones.
  5. Decision: keep and bank item 7; retire or rewrite item 12 after checking the key, the wording and whether a distractor is ambiguous.
Result Item 7: p = 0.70, D = 0.60 → keep. Item 12: p = 0.75, D = −0.10 → faulty, revise or retire.
Why this works

Difficulty alone is not a defect: easy items can be legitimate. Discrimination is the better test of whether an item is doing any work, and a negative value is almost always a signal of a broken key or ambiguous wording.

Review

Repairing a faulty multiple-choice item

Item: “Which of the following is NOT an example of formative assessment, and why is it important? (1 mark)” Options: (A) exit ticket (B) hinge question (C) annual board examination (D) all of the above.

  1. Split the double question: the stem asks both “which is not” and “why is it important” for one mark — two demands, one mark.
  2. Remove the negative framing and the cue: “NOT” plus “all of the above” lets partial knowledge score and confuses the reading.
  3. Fix the key: with “all of the above” present, more than one option can be defended, so the item has no single defensible answer.
  4. Rewrite: “A teacher checks understanding at the end of a lesson using a two-question exit ticket. This is best described as: (A) summative assessment (B) formative assessment (C) diagnostic assessment (D) norm-referenced assessment.”
Result A single clear stem, one defensible key (B), parallel options and no cueing — the rewritten item measures the intended distinction.
Why this works

Most faulty items fail on structure rather than subject knowledge: two demands in one stem, negative wording, implausible or overlapping options, or a key that is longer and more qualified than the distractors.

05

Practice

Classroom and test-room application

The left column is the professional standard; the right column lists the errors that cost marks and damage results.

do / avoid

✅ Do this

  • Write a one-page test plan before writing items.
  • Fill the table of specifications with marks before choosing item formats.
  • Write the marking scheme alongside the items, not after marking.
  • Have a colleague review items for clarity, key correctness and bias.
  • Pilot items and compute difficulty, discrimination and distractor data.
  • Bank good items with their statistics and retire faulty ones.

🚫 Avoid this

  • Do not begin with the questions; begin with the decision the test must support.
  • Do not assemble a paper from items that happened to be available.
  • Do not reuse an item that showed negative discrimination.
  • Do not pilot on the same learners who will sit the live test without adjusting for exposure.
  • Do not mark constructed responses without a scheme and moderation.
  • Do not let the paper total drift from the blueprint during assembly.

Exam traps

Misconceptions

Each myth is a plausible sentence that loses marks. Learn the reality column as the correction you can write.

4 corrections
MythItem analysis is only for large-scale standardized testing.
RealityCounting the proportion correct and comparing top and bottom groups takes minutes and is the most practical quality tool a classroom teacher has.
MythA difficult item is a bad item.
RealityDifficulty is not a defect by itself. A hard item with positive discrimination may be the most informative item on the paper; a very easy item can be legitimate for a core objective.
MythReview means checking the grammar.
RealityReview has three passes: editorial, technical (key, distractors, marks) and sensitivity (bias, access, reading load). Grammar is the smallest part.
MythOnce a test has been used, the work is finished.
RealityPost-test review is where the value is created: item statistics, marking discrepancies and learner responses determine what is kept, revised or retired.
07

Exam room

Exam-style questions

Answer first, then open the model. The chips show the command word, the mark value and the cognitive level being tested.

2 questions
Describe 8 marks Understand

Describe the stages of test development from planning to post-test review, stating the output of each stage.

Model answer

The first stage is planning, which produces a one-page test plan stating purpose, construct, outcomes, formats, time, marks and constraints. The second is the blueprint, which produces a table of specifications allocating marks across content areas and cognitive levels. The third is item writing and pre-test review, producing draft items with a key that have been checked editorially, technically and for sensitivity. The fourth is piloting, which produces item statistics — difficulty, discrimination and distractor data. The fifth is assembly, administration and scoring, producing the final paper, standardised instructions and a marking scheme supported by moderation. The sixth is post-test review, which produces revised items, an item bank entry and evidence about reliability and validity for the next cycle. The stages are iterative: review feeds back into item writing and the blueprint.

Apply 10 marks Apply

A 40-mark test has 12 items. Item 5 has a difficulty index of 0.92 and a discrimination index of 0.05; item 9 has a difficulty index of 0.38 and a discrimination index of 0.45. Explain what each set of figures means and state the action you would take, including any further checks.

Model answer

Item 5 is very easy (92 per cent correct) and barely discriminates (0.05), so it adds little information about who has mastered the content; it may be a well-intentioned confidence item, but with only 12 items it is occupying space that could carry more information. I would check its distractor data first: if the distractors were never chosen, they are not plausible and the item may be functioning as a three-option or even two-option item. Then I would either revise it into a more demanding item on the same objective or keep it as one deliberate easy opener if the blueprint requires coverage of that objective. Item 9 is hard (38 per cent correct) but discriminates strongly (0.45), which is exactly what a good challenging item looks like: stronger learners answer it and weaker learners do not. I would keep it, provided the difficulty is a consequence of the concept rather than of ambiguous wording, so I would check the key and read the item aloud with a colleague. In both cases I would record the decision and the statistics in the item bank so that future papers inherit the evidence.

Compress

Takeaways and revision

Revise these six lines the night before the paper, then tick the checklist once each one is retrievable without notes.

6 lines

Six things to remember

  1. Test development runs purpose → blueprint → items → review → pilot → assemble → score → analyse → revise.
  2. The table of specifications is the operational definition of content validity.
  3. Pre-test review has editorial, technical and sensitivity passes.
  4. Difficulty describes how many answered correctly; discrimination shows whether the item separates strong from weak.
  5. Negative or zero discrimination almost always means a faulty key, ambiguous wording or a trick item.
  6. Post-test review and an item bank turn one test into a improving departmental resource.
One-line summary

Test development is a staged process — plan, blueprint, write, review, pilot, assemble, score, analyse and revise — in which the blueprint secures validity and item analysis secures quality.

💡
Exam tip

In item-analysis questions, always show the formula and then interpret: p = number correct ÷ total; D = proportion correct in the top group − proportion correct in the bottom group. A negative D is the answer examiners want you to spot.
