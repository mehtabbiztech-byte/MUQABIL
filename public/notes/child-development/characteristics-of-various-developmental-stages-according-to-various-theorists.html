🧒 Child Development

Characteristics of Various Developmental Stages According to Various Theorists

A complete study guide for teacher recruitment tests, SIBA Teaching License Test, and other competitive exams
💡 Overview: Child development is the continuous process of physical, cognitive, emotional, social, moral, and language changes from conception through adolescence into adulthood. Development is generally orderly, but the rate and timing differ among individuals. Both heredity and environment influence development.

🏫 Major Developmental Theorists & Their Stages
🧩
Jean Piaget — Cognitive Development
How children think and learn · 1896–1980
Key ideas
Children are active learners who construct knowledge through interaction with their environment. Keywords: schemas, assimilation, accommodation, equilibration.
Four stages
StageAgeKey features
Sensorimotor0–2 yrsLearning through senses & actions; object permanence; early imitation
Preoperational2–7 yrsLanguage & symbolic thinking; egocentrism; centration; no conservation
Concrete Operational7–11 yrsLogical thinking about concrete objects; conservation; classification; reversibility
Formal Operational11+ yrsAbstract & hypothetical reasoning; systematic problem-solving; testing hypotheses
Classroom application
Use hands-on activities, concrete materials, discovery learning, and age-appropriate tasks. Move gradually from concrete to abstract.
📌 Exam tip: Piaget = cognitive development; Vygotsky = social interaction & scaffolding.
👥
Lev Vygotsky — Sociocultural Theory
Social interaction and language · 1896–1934
Key ideas
Cognitive development is strongly influenced by social interaction, culture, language, and guidance from more knowledgeable people.
Major concepts
ConceptExplanation
ZPDGap between independent performance and performance with guidance
MKOMore Knowledgeable Other — teacher, parent, or capable peer
ScaffoldingTemporary support gradually reduced as competence improves
Private speechSelf-directed speech that guides actions
Cultural toolsLanguage, symbols, writing used in learning
Classroom application
Encourage group work, peer teaching, guided practice, dialogue, and questions that help learners progress beyond what they can do alone.
📌 Exam tip: ZPD and social interaction are central to Vygotsky, not Piaget.
🔄
Erik Erikson — Psychosocial Development
Eight stages across the lifespan · 1902–1994
Key ideas
Each stage involves a central social or emotional challenge (crisis) that influences personality development.
Eight stages
AgeStage / conflictCharacteristic
Birth–1Trust vs. MistrustTrust develops with reliable care
1–3Autonomy vs. Shame/DoubtIndependence and self-control
3–6Initiative vs. GuiltExplores, plans actions
6–12Industry vs. InferiorityCompetence through schoolwork
12–18Identity vs. Role ConfusionExplores identity and values
Young adultIntimacy vs. IsolationClose committed relationships
Middle adultGenerativity vs. StagnationContributing to next generation
Late adultIntegrity vs. DespairReflecting with acceptance or regret
Educational importance
Build trust, give safe choices, encourage initiative, recognize effort, and support adolescent identity exploration.
📌 Exam tip: Industry vs. Inferiority = school age; Identity vs. Role Confusion = adolescence.
🧠
Sigmund Freud — Psychosexual Development
Unconscious drives and personality · 1856–1939
Key ideas
Early personality development is influenced by unconscious processes and psychosexual stages. Historically influential, though many claims lack modern scientific support.
Five stages
StageAgeFocus
Oral0–1Mouth; sucking and feeding
Anal1–3Elimination and toilet training
Phallic3–6Genital awareness; identification with parents
Latency6–pubertyImpulses dormant; social/academic interests
GenitalPuberty+Mature sexual interests and relationships
Fixation
Unresolved conflict at a stage may influence later personality.
📌 Exam tip: Freud = psychosexual; Erikson = psychosocial (more social & lifespan).
⚖️
Lawrence Kohlberg — Moral Development
Reasoning about right and wrong · 1927–1987
Key ideas
Moral reasoning develops through 3 levels and 6 stages, from self-interest to universal ethical principles.
Three levels & six stages
LevelStageCharacteristics
Pre-conventional1. Obedience & punishmentJudges by punishment or avoidance
2. Individualism & exchangePersonal benefit and fair exchange
Conventional3. Good interpersonal relationshipsSeeks approval; wants to be seen as good
4. Law and orderValues rules, duty, social order
Post-conventional5. Social contractLaws protect rights and may need reform
6. Universal ethical principlesJustice and human dignity
Classroom application
Discuss moral dilemmas, fairness, honesty, responsibility, and the reasons behind rules rather than relying only on punishment.
📌 Exam tip: Kohlberg describes moral reasoning, not necessarily behavior in every situation.
🤱
John Bowlby — Attachment Theory
Emotional bonds with caregivers · 1907–1990
Key ideas
Early emotional bonds between children and caregivers are crucial. Secure relationships provide a foundation for exploration and emotional regulation.
Attachment patterns (Ainsworth)
PatternCharacteristics
SecureUses caregiver as secure base; seeks comfort; soothed by reunion
Insecure-avoidantAvoids or minimizes contact on reunion
Insecure-resistant/ambivalentSeeks contact but resists comfort; remains distressed
DisorganizedContradictory, confused behavior in stressful situations
Classroom application
Provide a safe, predictable environment, respond sensitively to needs, and help children build trust and confidence.
📌 Exam tip: Bowlby = attachment theory; Ainsworth = Strange Situation procedure.
👀
Albert Bandura — Social Learning Theory
Observation and imitation · 1925–2021
Key ideas
Children learn many behaviors by observing and imitating others, even without direct reward.
Four processes of observational learning
  • ◆Attention: Notices the model's behavior.
  • ◆Retention: Remembers what was observed.
  • ◆Reproduction: Attempts to perform the behavior.
  • ◆Motivation: Has a reason to reproduce the behavior.
Important concepts
Modeling · Vicarious reinforcement · Self-efficacy · Reciprocal determinism.
Classroom application
Model respectful conduct, demonstrate problem-solving, and provide positive examples.
📌 Exam tip: Bandura = observational learning & Bobo doll experiment.
🔔
B. F. Skinner — Operant Conditioning
Reinforcement and punishment · 1904–1990
Key ideas
Consequences influence the likelihood of behavior occurring again.
Four types of consequences
ConceptMeaningEffect
Positive reinforcementAdd desirable consequenceIncrease behavior
Negative reinforcementRemove unpleasant conditionIncrease behavior
Positive punishmentAdd unpleasant consequenceReduce behavior
Negative punishmentRemove desirable privilegeReduce behavior
Other concepts
Shaping · Extinction · Schedules of reinforcement.
Classroom application
Set clear expectations, reinforce desired behaviors, give timely feedback, and use fair, proportionate consequences.
📌 Exam tip: Skinner = operant conditioning; Pavlov = classical conditioning.
🌱
Arnold Gesell — Maturational Theory
Biological maturation · 1880–1961
Key ideas
Development follows a broadly predictable sequence influenced by biological maturation. Children may reach milestones at different ages.
Main characteristics
  • ◆Predictable sequence of development.
  • ◆Biological maturation influences readiness.
  • ◆Children reach milestones at different ages.
  • ◆Physical/neurological development interacts with experience.
Classroom application
Consider developmental readiness, avoid forcing skills before a learner is ready, and recognize individual differences.
📌 Exam tip: Gesell = maturation & developmental readiness.
📋
Robert Havighurst — Developmental Tasks
Age-related social and personal tasks
Key ideas
Each period of life involves developmental tasks influenced by physical maturation, personal values, and social expectations.
Developmental tasks by period
PeriodTypical tasks
Early childhoodWalking, talking, basic concepts, right/wrong
Middle childhoodPhysical skills, friendships, reading/writing, conscience
AdolescenceMature peer relationships, emotional independence, preparing for work, forming values
Early adulthoodIntimate relationships, career, independent life
Middle adulthoodSupporting family, occupational responsibilities, contributing to society
Later adulthoodAdjusting to retirement, physical changes, changing roles
Classroom application
Design learning experiences that support age-appropriate skills, social relationships, independence, and responsibility.
📌 Exam tip: Havighurst = developmental tasks, not Piaget's stages of thinking.
🌐
Urie Bronfenbrenner — Ecological Systems Theory
Influence of family, school, and society
Key ideas
Development is influenced by interacting environmental systems, from immediate family relationships to wider social and cultural conditions.
Five systems
SystemExample
MicrosystemFamily, teachers, classmates — direct daily experiences
MesosystemConnections between home, school, and community
ExosystemIndirect influences, e.g., parent's workplace
MacrosystemCulture, social values, laws, economic conditions
ChronosystemChanges over time — life transitions, historical events
Classroom application
Understand that academic performance is influenced by family circumstances, school culture, community resources, and changes over time.
📌 Exam tip: Bronfenbrenner = ecological systems theory.

👶 Characteristics of Children at Different Age Levels
Infancy
Birth–2 years
  • Rapid physical and sensory development
  • Attachment to familiar caregivers
  • Exploration through touch, sight, sound, movement
  • Early communication and object permanence
Piaget Erikson Bowlby
Early Childhood
2–6 years
  • Rapid vocabulary development and imaginative play
  • Growing independence and self-control
  • Egocentric thinking and limited logical reasoning
  • Learning through play, imitation, exploration
Piaget Erikson Freud Vygotsky
Middle Childhood
6–11 years
  • Increasing logical thinking about concrete situations
  • Improved memory, attention, academic skills
  • Friendships, cooperation, rule awareness
  • Competence and achievement influence self-confidence
Piaget Erikson Kohlberg Bandura
Adolescence
12–18 years
  • Puberty and major physical changes
  • Increasing abstract reasoning and independent thought
  • Identity exploration and stronger peer influence
  • Developing values, judgment, emotional regulation
Erikson Piaget Kohlberg

🔑 Key Takeaway: Stage theories are frameworks for understanding development, not diagnostic checklists. Age ranges overlap, and children develop at different rates. Effective educators use these theories to match instruction to the learner's developmental level, while always remembering the individual child in front of them.
