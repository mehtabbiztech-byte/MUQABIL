Basics of Educational Psychology: Major Learning Theories

Five schools of thought — and how each shapes teaching
💡 Overview: Educational psychology provides the foundation for understanding how learning occurs and for developing effective instructional strategies. The field is built on several major schools of thought — Behaviorism, Cognitivism, Constructivism, Humanism, and Social Cognitive Theory (a critical bridge between behavioral and cognitive perspectives).

🔔 1. Behaviorism
🔔
Behaviorism
Core Premise: Learning is a relatively permanent change in observable behavior caused by experience and environmental stimuli. Behaviorists focus exclusively on what can be observed and measured, rejecting unobservable mental processes.
Key Theorists
Ivan Pavlov (Classical Conditioning), John B. Watson, Edward Thorndike (Connectionism), B.F. Skinner (Operant Conditioning)
Pavlov Watson Thorndike Skinner
View of Learning
Passive absorption of predefined knowledge, promoted by repetition and reinforcement. Learning is an automatic, habit-forming process.
Key Principles
Stimulus-Response (S-R) associations, Reinforcement (positive/negative), Punishment, Conditioning (classical & operant), Contiguity.
Instructional Focus
Teacher-centered; direct instruction, rote learning, repetitive drills, and programmed instruction.
📌 Classroom Application: Using rewards (praise, tokens) to encourage desired behaviors, breaking tasks into small steps, providing immediate feedback, and using clear behavior management systems (e.g., behavior contracts).
💾 2. Cognitivism
💾
Cognitivism
Core Premise: Learning is a relatively permanent change in mental associations due to experience. Cognitivism emerged as a reaction to behaviorism, shifting focus to internal mental processes such as reasoning, perception, insight, and information processing.
Key Theorists
Jean Piaget (Cognitive Development), David Ausubel (Meaningful Learning), Jerome Bruner (Discovery Learning), Noam Chomsky (Generative Linguistics)
Piaget Ausubel Bruner Chomsky
View of Learning
An active, internal process where learners receive, organize, store, and retrieve information. Learning is a change in mental behavior, not just observable action.
Key Principles
Information Processing (encoding, storage, retrieval), Schema Theory, Meaningful Learning (connecting to prior knowledge), Piaget's stages.
Instructional Focus
Teacher-guided but attention to cognitive processes. Emphasizes conceptual understanding, problem-solving strategies, and meaningful learning.
📌 Classroom Application: Using advance organizers (outlines, concept maps), teaching metacognitive strategies (self-monitoring), chunking information to avoid cognitive overload, and providing opportunities for discovery and insight.
🏗️ 3. Constructivism
🏗️
Constructivism
Core Premise: Learners are active agents who construct their own knowledge and understanding through experience, interaction, and reflection. Knowledge is not passively received but actively built upon existing schemas.
Key Theorists
Jean Piaget (Cognitive Constructivism), Lev Vygotsky (Social Constructivism), Jerome Bruner (Discovery Learning)
Piaget Vygotsky Bruner
View of Learning
A process of active construction and meaning-making. Learners interpret new information through the lens of their existing knowledge and experiences.
Key Principles
Active Construction, Social Interaction (Vygotsky), Contextual Learning, ZPD & Scaffolding, Assimilation & Accommodation (Piaget).
Instructional Focus
Student-centered; inquiry-based learning, problem-based learning (PBL), cooperative learning, discovery learning, experiential activities.
📌 Classroom Application: Encouraging group discussions and collaborative projects, designing hands-on activities, providing scaffolds for complex tasks, using real-world scenarios, and prompting students to reflect on their learning.

🔑 Key Distinction: Cognitive constructivism (Piaget) focuses on individual meaning-making, while social constructivism (Vygotsky) emphasizes social and cultural dimensions — language, collaboration, and guided interaction.
💖 4. Humanism
💖
Humanism
Core Premise: Learning is a holistic process involving the development of the whole person — intellectual, emotional, social, and spiritual potential. Humanists emphasize the affective domain, self-actualization, and the inherent drive toward growth.
Key Theorists
Abraham Maslow (Hierarchy of Needs), Carl Rogers (Learner-Centered Teaching, Unconditional Positive Regard)
Maslow Rogers
View of Learning
A personal, self-directed process aimed at self-actualization. Learning is most meaningful when personally relevant and involves both cognitive and emotional faculties.
Key Principles
Hierarchy of Needs, Self-Concept, Self-Direction, Unconditional Positive Regard, Student-Centeredness.
Instructional Focus
Student-centered; creating a safe, supportive, empathetic classroom environment. The teacher is a facilitator, not a director.
📌 Classroom Application: Ensuring students' basic needs are met, providing choice and autonomy, building strong teacher-student relationships based on empathy and trust, using cooperative learning, and focusing on personal growth rather than just academic outcomes.
🌉 5. Social Cognitive Theory (A Bridge)
🌉
Social Cognitive Theory
Core Premise: Learning occurs through observation, imitation, and modeling within a social context. Developed by Albert Bandura, this theory bridges behaviorism and cognitivism by acknowledging both external reinforcement and internal cognitive processes like self-efficacy and self-regulation.
Key Theorist
Albert Bandura
Bandura
View of Learning
A reciprocal interaction between personal factors (beliefs, expectations), behavior, and the environment — triadic reciprocal determinism.
Key Principles
Observational Learning (modeling), Self-Efficacy, Reciprocal Determinism, Self-Regulation, Vicarious Reinforcement.
Instructional Focus
Modeling desired behaviors, building student self-efficacy through mastery experiences and encouragement, and teaching self-regulation strategies.
📌 Classroom Application: Teachers modeling problem-solving and thinking aloud, using peer models to demonstrate success, providing specific and encouraging feedback to build confidence, and creating opportunities for students to set goals and monitor their own progress.
📊 Quick Comparison Table

💡 Key Takeaway for Educators: No single theory provides a complete picture of learning. Effective teaching often involves integrating principles from multiple theories — using behavioral reinforcement for foundational skills, cognitive strategies for complex understanding, constructivist activities for deep inquiry, humanistic approaches to build a supportive classroom culture, and social-cognitive modeling to build student confidence.
