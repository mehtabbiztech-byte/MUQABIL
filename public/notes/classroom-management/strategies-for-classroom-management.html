Strategies for Classroom Management

A tiered toolkit: prevent most disruption, correct calmly and early, and support the few who need more

⏱️ 9 min read 📚 7 sections 🔑 12 key terms 📝 10 MCQs · 4 CRQs · 1 ERQ

The big idea

Classroom management strategies work best as a tiered system rather than a list of tricks. Tier one is proactive: relationships, expectations, routines, engaging instruction and physical design prevent most disruption. Tier two is corrective and low-key: proximity, signals, private reminders and choices stop emerging behaviour before it escalates. Tier three is intensive and individual: functional assessment, behaviour plans and partnership with families and specialists. The skill is choosing the least intrusive strategy that will actually work.

What you will master

  1. Organise management strategies into proactive, corrective and intensive tiers.
  2. Choose the least intrusive intervention that will actually work and follow through once.
  3. Replace a sanctions-only plan with functional assessment and a taught replacement behaviour.

Foundations

Core concepts

Each card isolates one idea examiners test. Read the definition first, then the In practice line — that is where scenario questions begin.

🛡️ 01

Tier 1 — Proactive strategies

Prevention includes co-constructed expectations, taught routines, high but realistic expectations, strong relationships, engaging and appropriately challenging instruction, purposeful movement around the room, and positive narration of what learners are doing well.

In practice

Spend the first two weeks teaching routines and reinforcing them; the investment returns all year.

👀 02

Kounin’s group-management techniques

Withitness (noticing before it spreads), overlapping (managing two things at once), smoothness (no jerky transitions), momentum (no dead time), group alerting (keeping everyone accountable) and format variety reduce disruption without correction.

In practice

Keep the whole class accountable by asking a question, pausing, then calling on a learner at random rather than only on volunteers.

🤫 03

Low-key corrective strategies

The least intrusive options include proximity, eye contact, a gesture, a name plus brief instruction, planned ignoring of low-level attention-seeking paired with praise for the desired behaviour, and private reminders. These preserve dignity and avoid escalation.

In practice

Hold up two fingers and point to the rule on the wall instead of announcing a correction across the room.

⚖️ 04

Consequences and behaviourist tools

Logical consequences should be related, respectful, reasonable and reliably applied. Positive reinforcement — specific praise, recognition, token systems, group contingencies — builds the behaviour you want. Behaviour contracts and self-monitoring suit individual cases.

In practice

Praise the process (“you waited and then asked”) rather than the person (“good boy”) so the behaviour is repeatable.

🗣️ 05

Communication and de-escalation

Calm voice, neutral body language, I-statements (“I need the talking to stop so the group can hear”), limited choices (“you can work here or at the front table”), and time to comply reduce power struggles. Ginott’s congruent communication avoids sarcasm and labelling.

In practice

Give a choice plus a short period to comply; then follow through calmly rather than repeating the demand louder.

🧩 06

Tier 3 — Intensive individual support

When behaviour persists, use ABC data, a functional behaviour assessment, an individual behaviour plan with replacement behaviours, self-monitoring, mentor check-ins, and collaboration with families, counsellors and specialists. Restorative repair follows incidents.

In practice

Write a plan that teaches a replacement behaviour (“ask for a break card”) rather than only listing sanctions.

Precision

Key vocabulary

Be able to define each term in one sentence. Bold terms below also appear highlighted inside the notes, so you can meet them in context.

Tier 1 (proactive) strategies
Prevention through relationships, expectations, routines, engaging instruction and environment design.
Tier 2 (corrective) strategies
Low-key, early responses to emerging behaviour, such as proximity, signals and private reminders.
Tier 3 (intensive) support
Individual assessment and planning for persistent, high-impact behaviour.
Proximity control
Moving physically near a learner to redirect behaviour without speaking.
Planned ignoring
Deliberately withholding attention from minor attention-seeking behaviour while reinforcing the desired alternative.
Positive narration
Describing aloud what learners are doing correctly.
Logical consequence
A consequence that is related, respectful, reasonable and reliably applied.
De-escalation
Reducing arousal through calm tone, neutral body language, limited choices and time to comply.
I-statement
A statement describing impact from the speaker’s perspective without blaming.
Functional behaviour assessment (FBA)
A process of identifying the function of a behaviour so that a replacement can be taught.
Replacement behaviour
An acceptable behaviour that serves the same function as the problem behaviour.
Behaviour contract
A written agreement specifying expectations, supports and consequences.

04

Practice

Classroom application

What to do, and what to avoid, in a real classroom. The left column is the standard to teach; the right column lists the errors that create management problems.

✅ Do this

  • Choose the least intrusive strategy likely to work, then follow through calmly.
  • Narrate the positive: describe what learners are doing correctly three to four times as often as you correct.
  • Use proximity and private reminders before public correction.
  • Make consequences related, respectful, reasonable and consistent.
  • Offer limited choices rather than ultimatums.
  • Use ABC data and a replacement behaviour for persistent cases.

🚫 Avoid this

  • Do not escalate in public; it invites a power struggle you cannot win.
  • Do not use sarcasm, humiliation or collective punishment.
  • Do not repeat a demand louder without follow-through.
  • Do not remove break or lunch time as a routine sanction.
  • Do not confiscate support (scaffolds, movement, sensory tools) as a punishment.
  • Do not write behaviour plans that list only sanctions and no replacement behaviour.
05

Exam traps

Common misconceptions

Each misconception below is a plausible-sounding statement that loses marks. Learn the reality column as a correction you can write.

Myth

“You should never smile before Christmas.”

✔ Reality

Warmth and structure are complementary. Coldness does not create respect; predictable expectations plus genuine care do.

Myth

“Consequences must be severe to be effective.”

✔ Reality

Consistency matters far more than severity. Severe consequences increase resentment and avoidance while teaching nothing.

Myth

“Praise will spoil learners.”

✔ Reality

Specific, process-focused praise increases the behaviour it names and supports self-efficacy. Vague or controlling praise is what becomes hollow.

Myth

“A behaviour plan is a list of punishments.”

✔ Reality

A functional behaviour plan identifies the function of behaviour and teaches a replacement that meets the same need, alongside environmental adjustments and consequences.

Compress

Key takeaways

Revise these lines the night before the paper — they compress the whole note into recall-ready statements.

Six things to remember

  1. Most disruption is prevented by proactive Tier 1 practice, not corrected after the fact.
  2. Kounin’s techniques — withitness, overlapping, smoothness, momentum, group alerting — remain the practical core.
  3. Use the least intrusive correction that will work; escalate only when lower tiers fail.
  4. Consequences should be related, respectful, reasonable and reliably applied.
  5. Praise and reinforce the behaviour you want far more than you punish the behaviour you don’t.
  6. Persistent behaviour needs functional assessment and a replacement behaviour, not more sanctions.
One-line summary

Effective management is tiered: prevent through relationships, routines and engaging teaching; correct early and quietly; and support persistent behaviour with functional assessment and a taught replacement.

💡
Exam tip

The exam phrase “least intrusive intervention” signals a hierarchy answer: proximity and non-verbal cues first, private reminder next, logical consequence after that, intensive support last.
