Types of Test Questions

Choose the item type that matches the learning you claim to measure — then write it properly

⏱️ 10 min read 📚 7 sections 🔑 12 key terms 📝 10 MCQs · 4 CRQs · 1 ERQ

The big idea

Every test item belongs to one of two families. Selected-response items — multiple choice, true or false, matching and completion — give the learner options to choose from, so marking is quick and perfectly consistent, but the learner can guess and cannot generate an answer. Constructed-response items — short answer and extended response — require the learner to produce the answer, so they can assess organisation and reasoning, but marking becomes slower and less reliable. The professional skill lies in matching item type to cognitive demand, then writing items carefully: a well-designed multiple-choice question can test application and analysis, while a badly written essay can test only memory. A table of specifications and simple item analysis are what separate a defensible test from a collection of questions.

What you will master

  1. Classify items as selected-response or constructed-response, and state the strengths and limitations of each.
  2. Write multiple-choice items with a clear stem, one defensible key and plausible misconception-based distractors.
  3. Evaluate test quality using a table of specifications, difficulty index and discrimination index.

Foundations

Core concepts

Each card isolates one idea examiners test. Read the definition first, then the In practice line — that is where scenario questions begin.

🔘 01

Selected-response items

Selected-response items include multiple choice, true or false, matching and completion. They sample content widely in a short time, are marked objectively and consistently, and can reach higher-order thinking when the options require discrimination between close alternatives. Their limits are guessing, cueing, and the inability to show how a learner reached an answer.

In practice

Write distractors from real misconceptions you have seen in learners’ work, not from invented wrong answers.

✍️ 02

Constructed-response items

Constructed-response items require learners to produce language, working or an artefact: short answers, definitions of terms in their own words, explanations, essays and extended responses. They can assess reasoning, organisation and originality, but they demand a marking scheme or rubric, take longer to mark, and are vulnerable to marker inconsistency.

In practice

Mark a sample of five scripts twice, a day apart, and see whether your own marks agree.

🎯 03

Objective versus subjective is about marking, not difficulty

An objective test is one whose scoring is independent of the marker’s judgement; a subjective test requires judgement. ‘Objective’ does not mean easy or factual, and ‘subjective’ does not mean unreliable — essays can be marked consistently with an analytic rubric and moderation, while a poorly keyed objective item can be contested.

In practice

Use the terms only to describe scoring, and say what evidence supports the reliability of your marking.

🧩 04

Anatomy and rules of a good multiple-choice item

A multiple-choice item has a stem presenting the problem and four options, of which one is the key and the rest are distractors. The stem should pose one clear problem in positive wording; options should be parallel in length and grammar, mutually exclusive, and plausible; there should be no cues such as ‘all of the above’ or a longest option that is always correct.

In practice

Check that every distractor could attract a learner holding a specific misconception — if not, replace it.

📐 05

Cognitive demand and the table of specifications

Item type does not determine cognitive level: a multiple-choice item can ask for recall or for evaluation. A table of specifications maps items across content areas and cognitive levels so that the paper reflects the taught curriculum and its weighting. Without it, tests drift towards whichever items are easiest to write — usually recall.

In practice

Before writing, draw a grid of topics × levels and allocate marks; then write to the grid.

📊 06

Item analysis: difficulty and discrimination

The difficulty index is the proportion of learners answering correctly; values near 1 are easy and near 0 are hard, and most items should sit in a moderate band. The discrimination index compares performance of the top and bottom groups: an item that strong learners get right and weak learners get wrong is discriminating well, while a negative or zero value signals a faulty key or a trick item.

In practice

After every class test, compute both indices for five items and rewrite the worst one.

02

Precision

Key vocabulary

Be able to define each term in one sentence. Bold terms below also appear highlighted inside the notes, so you can meet them in context.

Selected-response item
An item in which the learner chooses the answer from given options, such as multiple choice or matching.
Constructed-response item
An item in which the learner produces the answer, such as a short answer or an essay.
Objective test
A test whose scoring does not depend on marker judgement.
Subjective test
A test that requires marker judgement to score, such as an essay examination.
Stem
The part of a multiple-choice item that presents the problem or question.
Distractor
An incorrect option designed to attract learners holding a specific misconception.
Key
The correct option in a selected-response item.
Difficulty index
The proportion of learners who answer an item correctly.
Discrimination index
The difference in success on an item between high-achieving and low-achieving groups.
Table of specifications
A blueprint allocating items or marks across content and cognitive levels.
Marking scheme
Documented criteria and mark allocation used to score constructed responses consistently.
Halo effect
A marker’s tendency to let an overall impression influence scores on separate criteria.

04

Practice

Classroom application

What to do, and what to avoid, in a real classroom. The left column is the standard to teach; the right column lists the errors that cost marks and learning.

✅ Do this

  • Write the table of specifications before writing any item.
  • Keep one idea per item and put the problem in the stem, not the options.
  • Build distractors from misconceptions you have actually observed.
  • Use plausible, parallel and mutually exclusive options of similar length.
  • Provide a marking scheme or rubric for every constructed-response item.
  • Run a quick item analysis and revise or retire weak items.

🚫 Avoid this

  • Do not use ‘all of the above’ or ‘none of the above’ as a routine fourth option.
  • Do not use double negatives or unnecessarily complex wording in the stem.
  • Do not make the correct option consistently the longest or most qualified.
  • Do not test trivia in a paper that claims to assess understanding.
  • Do not write essay questions without criteria — learners cannot guess the standard.
  • Do not reuse a faulty item because it is familiar; a negative discrimination index means it is broken.
05

Exam traps

Common misconceptions

Each misconception below is a plausible-sounding statement that loses marks. Learn the reality column as a correction you can write.

Myth

“Multiple-choice questions can only test recall.”

✔ Reality

Well-written items can test application, analysis and evaluation by requiring learners to choose between close alternatives, interpret data or predict an outcome. Item type does not fix cognitive demand; the question does.

Myth

“Essay questions are more valid because they are harder.”

✔ Reality

Essays can assess complex reasoning, but without a rubric and moderation they are less reliable. Validity depends on alignment with the outcome, not on length.

Myth

“Objective tests are always fairer than subjective tests.”

✔ Reality

Objectivity of scoring is not the same as fairness. A keyed item can be ambiguous or test the wrong thing, and guessing introduces error that essay tasks do not have.

Myth

“A test with a good spread of marks is a good test.”

✔ Reality

A spread can come from trick items and ambiguous wording. Quality requires a table of specifications, sound items and positive discrimination.

Compress

Key takeaways

Revise these lines the night before the paper — they compress the whole note into recall-ready statements.

Six things to remember

  1. Selected-response items give objectivity and coverage; constructed-response items give depth and reasoning.
  2. ‘Objective’ and ‘subjective’ describe marking, not difficulty.
  3. A table of specifications controls content coverage and cognitive demand.
  4. Good multiple-choice items have a clear stem and misconception-based distractors.
  5. Difficulty and discrimination indices tell you which items to keep, revise or discard.
  6. Reliability in constructed-response marking comes from rubrics, anchors and moderation.
One-line summary

Test questions divide into selected-response and constructed-response types, each with distinct strengths, and a defensible test matches item type to cognitive demand, uses a table of specifications and checks items through difficulty and discrimination analysis.

💡
Exam tip

Watch for the words ‘objective’ and ‘subjective’: they describe how an item is scored, not how hard it is. An item asking learners to select the best interpretation of a poem is still objective if it is multiple choice.
