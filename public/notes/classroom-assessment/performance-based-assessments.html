Performance-Based Assessment

Ask learners to do the thing, not just describe it — then judge the doing with a rubric

⏱️ 10 min read 📚 7 sections 🔑 12 key terms 📝 10 MCQs · 4 CRQs · 1 ERQ

The big idea

Performance-based assessment asks learners to demonstrate knowledge and skill by producing a performance or a product against explicit criteria. It is the natural way to assess outcomes that cannot be captured by a written test: conducting an experiment, repairing a circuit, delivering a presentation, resolving a case, designing a model or holding a conversation. Because the task is usually authentic — resembling what practitioners actually do — it assesses transfer rather than recall. The price of that validity is a set of practical problems: reliability of judgement, time, resources and fairness of access. Those problems are solved by design — clear criteria, rubric levels, process evidence, checkpoints and moderation — not by avoiding performance tasks.

What you will master

  1. Define performance-based and authentic assessment, and distinguish them from paper-and-pencil testing.
  2. Design a performance task with explicit criteria, process evidence and manageable logistics.
  3. Explain how rubrics, anchors and moderation protect reliability in performance assessment.

Foundations

Core concepts

Each card isolates one idea examiners test. Read the definition first, then the In practice line — that is where scenario questions begin.

🛠️ 01

What makes an assessment performance-based

In performance-based assessment the learner constructs a response, product or demonstration rather than selecting from options, and the assessment judges the quality of that production against criteria. The task is usually multi-step, takes time, and requires the application of knowledge in a context. The judged evidence may be the product, the process, or both.

In practice

Write the task as a verb a learner could perform in front of you — explain, build, measure, demonstrate, debate.

🌍 02

Authenticity: resemblance to real work

Authentic assessment uses tasks, audiences and constraints that resemble real practice: writing to an actual audience, investigating a local problem, following laboratory conventions, presenting to a panel. Authenticity increases the likelihood that success transfers beyond the classroom, and it raises motivation because the purpose is visible.

In practice

Give the task a real audience and a real constraint; both raise authenticity more cheaply than technology does.

🧾 03

Types of performance task

Common forms include experiments and practicals, projects and models, presentations and demonstrations, debates and role plays, simulations, exhibitions, fieldwork and portfolios. They differ in whether the main evidence is a product (a model, report, artefact) or a process (an investigation, a performance), and in how much can be observed live.

In practice

Choose the form that makes the target skill unavoidable — a debate if the outcome is argumentation, a practical if it is technique.

📋 04

Designing the task: criteria before activity

A defensible task specifies the outcome being assessed, the conditions (time, resources, collaboration rules), the evidence to be collected and the criteria for success. Criteria must come from the learning outcome, and the rubric must be written before learners begin so that teaching, feedback and judgement all point at the same target.

In practice

Draft the rubric first, then write the task sheet; if a criterion cannot be observed, change the task.

🔍 05

Process evidence and checkpoints

Because performance tasks unfold over time, evidence should be captured at checkpoints: a plan, a draft, a progress log, a photograph of a stage, a brief conference. Checkpoints reduce the risk of a final product being produced by someone else, allow early feedback, and give the teacher evidence of process rather than only product.

In practice

Schedule three short checkpoints and keep a one-line record of each learner’s progress at every one.

⚖️ 06

Reliability, fairness and practical constraints

Performance judgements are vulnerable to subjectivity, halo effects, order effects and inconsistent conditions. Reliability is protected by analytic rubrics, anchor scripts at each level, moderation, and standardised conditions. Fairness requires attention to resources, language demands and access needs, so that the task measures the intended skill rather than the learner’s access to materials.

In practice

Moderate one task with a colleague each term, scoring three scripts independently before discussing them.

02

Precision

Key vocabulary

Be able to define each term in one sentence. Bold terms below also appear highlighted inside the notes, so you can meet them in context.

Performance-based assessment
Assessment in which learners demonstrate skills by producing a performance or product judged against criteria.
Authentic assessment
Assessment using tasks, audiences and constraints that resemble real-world practice.
Rubric
A scoring tool listing criteria and describing performance levels for each.
Criterion
A dimension of performance that is judged, derived from the learning outcome.
Level descriptor
The statement describing what performance looks like at a particular level of a criterion.
Anchor
An exemplar of performance used to calibrate marking at a particular level.
Process evidence
Documentation of how a product was produced, such as drafts, logs or observations.
Checkpoint
A scheduled moment in a long task at which evidence is reviewed and feedback given.
Moderation
Comparing and agreeing judgements across assessors to ensure consistent standards.
Halo effect
The influence of an overall impression on judgements of separate criteria.
Reliability
Consistency of judgements across assessors, occasions and tasks.
Access arrangements
Adjustments that remove barriers so that a task measures the intended skill.

04

Practice

Classroom application

What to do, and what to avoid, in a real classroom. The left column is the standard to teach; the right column lists the errors that cost marks and learning.

✅ Do this

  • Derive criteria from the learning outcome before designing the task.
  • Write the rubric and share it with learners at the start.
  • Collect process evidence through scheduled checkpoints.
  • Use anchors or exemplars to show what each level looks like.
  • Standardise conditions and note any variation for fairness.
  • Moderate a sample of judgements with a colleague each term.

🚫 Avoid this

  • Do not assess a product you cannot observe being made without process evidence.
  • Do not score confidence, neatness or compliance as if they were the target skill.
  • Do not use a single holistic impression when the outcome has several criteria.
  • Do not set a task that requires expensive materials learners may not have.
  • Do not let group work hide individual contribution — assess it separately.
  • Do not assume performance assessment is automatically valid; a badly designed task is still invalid.
05

Exam traps

Common misconceptions

Each misconception below is a plausible-sounding statement that loses marks. Learn the reality column as a correction you can write.

Myth

“Performance-based assessment is more valid, so it needs no criteria.”

✔ Reality

Validity comes from alignment plus defensible judgement. Without explicit criteria, a performance task becomes an impression, and impressions vary with the judge, the order and the learner’s confidence.

Myth

“Performance tasks cannot be reliable.”

✔ Reality

Reliability is lower by default but can be raised substantially through analytic rubrics, anchors, standardised conditions and moderation. Essays were once thought unmarkable for the same reason.

Myth

“A good product proves the learning.”

✔ Reality

Products can be bought, copied or produced by someone else. Process evidence — drafts, logs, checkpoints, observation — is what connects the product to the learner.

Myth

“Group projects assess collaboration and content equally well.”

✔ Reality

A group product does not show individual content mastery. Collaboration and content need separate criteria, and individual accountability needs a separate evidence point.

Compress

Key takeaways

Revise these lines the night before the paper — they compress the whole note into recall-ready statements.

Six things to remember

  1. Performance-based assessment judges what learners can do, not only what they can recall.
  2. Authenticity — real audience, real constraints — raises transfer and motivation.
  3. Criteria and rubrics must be derived from the outcome and written before the task.
  4. Process evidence and checkpoints protect authenticity and enable feedback.
  5. Reliability comes from analytic rubrics, anchors and moderation.
  6. Fairness requires attention to resources, language and access arrangements.
One-line summary

Performance-based assessment asks learners to produce a performance or product judged against explicit criteria, trading some reliability for much greater validity on complex outcomes, and recovering reliability through rubrics, anchors, process evidence and moderation.

💡
Exam tip

If a scenario describes a learner doing something — conducting, presenting, building, debating — the answer is performance-based assessment; if it describes selecting or writing under exam conditions, it is a test. Look for the words ‘product’, ‘performance’ and ‘criteria’.
