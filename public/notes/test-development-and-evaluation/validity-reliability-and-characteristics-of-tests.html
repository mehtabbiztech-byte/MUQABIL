Validity, Reliability and Characteristics of Tests

Reliability tells you the score is stable; validity tells you the score means what you claim — you need both, in that order

  • ⏱️ 15 min read
  • 📚 8 sections
  • 🔑 12 key terms
  • 🧩 3 worked examples
  • 📝 10 MCQs · 4 CRQs · 1 ERQ

The big idea

Validity is the extent to which a test measures what it claims to measure for the purpose intended; reliability is the consistency of its results. They are related but not interchangeable: reliability is necessary for validity, and validity is capped by reliability, yet a test can be perfectly reliable and still measure the wrong thing. Both are properties of interpretations and uses, not of paper. Alongside them sit the other characteristics of a good test — objectivity, usability, fairness, discrimination, comprehensiveness and adequate norms. In practice these are ensured by a table of specifications, careful item writing, a marking scheme or rubric, piloting, item analysis, moderation and honest reporting that acknowledges measurement error.

What you will master

  1. Distinguish the main types of validity and reliability and the evidence each requires.
  2. Explain the relationship between the two, including why reliability limits validity.
  3. Apply the characteristics of a good test to evaluate and improve a real paper.

Foundations

Core concepts

Six ideas carry this topic. Each card gives the definition, the wording examiners use, and what the idea looks like in a real test.

6 cards
🎯 01 / 06

Validity: what the score means

Validity is the degree to which a test measures what it is intended to measure, and it applies to the interpretation of scores rather than to the paper itself. The main forms are content (representative coverage of the domain), construct (measuring the intended trait rather than something else), criterion (correlating with a concurrent or future criterion) and face (appearing appropriate to learners and users). Each form needs different evidence.

Exam wording“Validity is the extent to which a test measures what it is intended to measure.”

In practiceWrite the validity claim in one sentence — “this test measures X for purpose Y” — then list the evidence you actually have for it.
📏 02 / 06

Reliability: consistency of the score

Reliability is the consistency of results across occasions, forms and markers. It is estimated by test-retest (same test, two occasions), parallel forms (equivalent tests), internal consistency (split-half, KR-20, Cronbach’s alpha) and inter-rater agreement. A reliability coefficient runs from 0 to 1; higher is better, and the standard error of measurement converts it into a band around an individual score.

Exam wording“Reliability refers to the consistency of a test in producing comparable results on repeated occasions.”

In practiceReport scores with a band, not a point: a mark of 62 with an SEM of 3 means 59–65.
🔗 03 / 06

The relationship: necessary, not sufficient

Reliability is necessary for validity because an inconsistent test cannot support any stable interpretation, and validity is bounded by reliability: the correlation of a test with any criterion cannot exceed the square root of its reliability. But reliability does not guarantee validity — a reliable spelling test is still an invalid measure of essay writing. Conversely, a test can be aimed at the right construct yet be unreliable if it is short or inconsistently marked.

Exam wording“Reliability is a necessary but not sufficient condition for validity.”

In practiceWhen a test shows poor validity, check reliability first; when it shows good reliability, do not assume validity.
🧾 04 / 06

The other characteristics of a good test

Beyond the two central properties, a good test is objective (scoring is independent of marker judgement), usable or practical (time, cost, administration), fair (no bias against groups), discriminating (it separates secure from insecure performance), comprehensive (proportionate coverage) and, where relevant, supported by adequate norms for the group being compared.

Exam wording“A good test is valid, reliable, objective, practical, fair and comprehensive.”

In practiceAudit a recent paper on all seven characteristics and write one improvement for the weakest.
🧩 05 / 06

What raises and lowers reliability

Reliability increases with test length (more items sample better), a spread of difficulty that produces variance, homogeneous items measuring one construct, standard conditions, clear instructions and consistent scoring with rubrics and moderation. It falls with very short tests, ambiguous items, guessing, fatigue, poor administration and marker drift.

Exam wording“Other things being equal, a longer test is more reliable than a shorter one.”

In practiceBefore lengthening a test, check whether the extra items measure the same construct; length without homogeneity adds noise.
🔧 06 / 06

What raises and lowers validity

Validity is improved by a blueprint tied to outcomes, removing construct-irrelevant difficulty (reading load, cultural references), writing unambiguous items, piloting and reviewing with colleagues, and aligning the scoring rubric with the outcome. It is threatened by poor alignment, biased content, coaching that inflates scores without learning, and decisions made beyond the range the test can support.

Exam wording“Construct-irrelevant variance lowers validity because the score reflects something other than the intended construct.”

In practiceFor every item ask: what else could a learner need to answer this correctly, and is that part of the construct?

04

Application

Worked examples

Three walkthroughs take the definitions to the level the paper asks for: a calculation, a judgement and a repair.

3 walkthroughs
Calculation

Split-half reliability with the Spearman–Brown correction

A 20-item test is split into odd and even items. The correlation between the two halves is 0.64. Estimate the reliability of the full test.

  1. Recognise the problem: the observed 0.64 is the reliability of a 10-item half, not of the 20-item test.
  2. Apply the Spearman–Brown formula: r_full = 2r ÷ (1 + r), where r is the half-test correlation.
  3. Substitute: 2 × 0.64 ÷ (1 + 0.64) = 1.28 ÷ 1.64 ≈ 0.78.
  4. Interpret: 0.78 is acceptable for a classroom test but not for high-stakes individual decisions; the standard error of measurement would still be substantial.
Result Full-test reliability ≈ 0.78, obtained by correcting the half-test correlation upward.
Why this works

Reliability rises with length. Reporting the uncorrected half-test value would understate the test’s consistency, which is why split-half estimates must always be corrected.

Judgement

Reading a reliability coefficient honestly

A test has a reliability of 0.62 and a standard deviation of 8. A learner scores 54; the pass mark is 50. Should the school treat the learner as having passed?

  1. Translate reliability into error: SEM = SD × √(1 − r) = 8 × √0.38 ≈ 4.9.
  2. Build the band: the learner’s true score is likely to lie within roughly ±1 SEM, so about 49 to 59.
  3. Compare with the cut-off: the band straddles the pass mark of 50, so the pass decision is not secure on this evidence.
  4. Act defensibly: use a second source of evidence — classwork, a practical, a retest — rather than deciding on a score that is within error of the boundary.
Result SEM ≈ 4.9; the band 49–59 crosses the cut-off, so the decision needs corroborating evidence.
Why this works

Reliability coefficients are not administrative decoration: they determine how finely a score may be used. Decisions near a boundary are the most sensitive to error.

Repair

Diagnosing a validity problem

A mathematics test correlates strongly with a reading-comprehension test (r = 0.71) but only weakly with a practical numeracy task (r = 0.28). Items contain long word problems.

  1. Read the pattern: high correlation with reading and low with numeracy suggests the test is measuring reading as much as mathematics.
  2. Identify construct-irrelevant variance: long, complex stems add a reading demand that is not part of the mathematics construct.
  3. Check the blueprint: count how many items require multi-sentence interpretation before any calculation.
  4. Repair: shorten stems, separate context from the operation, allow a reader where appropriate, and add items that assess the same operations with minimal reading.
Result The test is reliable but not valid for mathematics; stem length and reading load are the likely cause, and the repair is textual, not statistical.
Why this works

Correlation patterns are validity evidence. A test that tracks the wrong construct will predict the wrong things, however consistent its scores are.

Practice

Classroom and test-room application

The left column is the professional standard; the right column lists the errors that cost marks and damage results.

do / avoid

✅ Do this

  • State the validity claim and the purpose before collecting any evidence.
  • Build the paper from a table of specifications so content validity is designed in.
  • Estimate reliability with the method that fits the test: retest, parallel forms, split-half or inter-rater.
  • Report scores with a standard error of measurement band when decisions matter.
  • Train markers, use a rubric and moderate a sample to raise inter-rater reliability.
  • Audit the paper against objectivity, usability, fairness, discrimination, comprehensiveness and norms.

🚫 Avoid this

  • Do not treat validity and reliability as synonyms or as opposites.
  • Do not claim validity on the basis of a single correlation or of expert approval alone.
  • Do not report a split-half coefficient without the Spearman–Brown correction.
  • Do not use a score within one standard error of a cut-off to make a high-stakes decision alone.
  • Do not raise reliability by lengthening the test with items that measure something else.
  • Do not describe a paper as objective when it contains essay items without a rubric.

Exam traps

Misconceptions

Each myth is a plausible sentence that loses marks. Learn the reality column as the correction you can write.

4 corrections
MythA reliable test is a valid test.
RealityReliability is consistency; validity is meaning. A test can consistently measure the wrong construct, and a valid intention can be undermined by unreliable marking.
MythValidity is a fixed property of the paper.
RealityValidity belongs to interpretations and uses. The same scores may be valid for describing class progress and invalid for certifying individual competence or predicting future success.
MythContent validity is guaranteed by covering every chapter.
RealityCoverage must be proportionate to emphasis and cognitive level, not merely present. One recall item per chapter can still misrepresent the curriculum.
MythReliability can be judged by looking at the paper.
RealityReliability is estimated empirically — by retest, parallel forms, internal consistency or inter-rater agreement — and different methods answer different questions.

Exam room

Exam-style questions

Answer first, then open the model. The chips show the command word, the mark value and the cognitive level being tested.

2 questions
Explain 8 marks Understand

Explain the relationship between reliability and validity, and why reliability is described as necessary but not sufficient.

Model answer

Reliability is the consistency of a test’s results across occasions, forms and markers; validity is the extent to which the test measures what it claims for the intended purpose. Reliability is necessary because an inconsistent instrument cannot support a stable interpretation: if the same learner would score very differently on a retest or under a different marker, no claim about their achievement is defensible. Reliability also limits validity mathematically, since a test’s correlation with a criterion cannot exceed the square root of its reliability. But reliability is not sufficient: a spelling test marked with a perfect key is highly consistent yet invalid as a measure of essay-writing competence, because it measures a different construct. Validity therefore requires separate evidence about what the scores mean — content coverage, construct relationships or criterion agreement — while reliability only establishes that the numbers are stable.

Evaluate 10 marks Evaluate

A school uses a 15-item multiple-choice test with a reliability of 0.55 to decide which learners enter an advanced stream. Evaluate the decision.

Model answer

The decision is not defensible on this evidence. A reliability of 0.55 means that a substantial proportion of the variance in scores is error, and with a short 15-item test the standard error of measurement will be large relative to the spread of scores. Learners near the cut-off cannot be distinguished reliably, so placement will partly reflect measurement error. Validity is also questionable: if the outcome for the advanced stream involves reasoning or extended work, a multiple-choice test may not represent it, and its correlation with later performance is unknown. Fairness concerns arise because a single short test gives no opportunity for access arrangements or alternative evidence, and the consequence is high for the learner. A defensible process would lengthen and broaden the test from a blueprint, raise reliability through more items and moderation, combine the score with teacher assessment and a performance task, set a band around the cut-off where additional evidence is collected, and review the placement decisions against later outcomes to establish predictive validity.

Takeaways and revision

Revise these six lines the night before the paper, then tick the checklist once each one is retrievable without notes.

6 lines

Six things to remember

  1. Validity concerns what the score means; reliability concerns how consistent it is.
  2. Reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity, and validity is capped by reliability.
  3. Content, construct, criterion and face validity each require different evidence.
  4. Reliability is estimated by retest, parallel forms, internal consistency or inter-rater agreement.
  5. The standard error of measurement turns reliability into a band that should inform decisions.
  6. A good test is also objective, usable, fair, discriminating, comprehensive and appropriately normed.
One-line summary

Validity is the meaning of a score and reliability is its consistency; reliability bounds validity, and both must be designed in through blueprints, careful items, rubrics, piloting and moderation rather than assumed.

💡
Exam tip

If a question supplies a reliability coefficient and a standard deviation, it is inviting an SEM calculation: SEM = SD × √(1 − r). Show the formula, then interpret the band — that interpretation is where most marks sit.
