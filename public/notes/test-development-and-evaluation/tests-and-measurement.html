Tests and Measurement

A test is the instrument; measurement is the scale it reads — and the number only means something once you know the scale

  • ⏱️ 16 min read
  • 📚 8 sections
  • 🔑 12 key terms
  • 🧩 3 worked examples
  • 📝 10 MCQs · 4 CRQs · 1 ERQ

The big idea

A test is a systematic procedure for sampling behaviour under standard conditions and scoring it by a fixed rule. Measurement is the step that turns that behaviour into a number — marks, levels or ranks — according to a rule. In education the attributes being measured are latent: achievement, aptitude and attitude cannot be seen directly, so every score is an inference from a sample of performance and therefore carries error. What you may legitimately claim about a score depends on its scale of measurement (nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio) and on how it is interpreted — as a percentile rank, a z-score or a stanine. Most avoidable errors in this topic come from treating a test as if it measured directly, or a percentile as if it were a percentage.

What you will master

  1. Define test and measurement precisely, and explain why educational measurement is always indirect.
  2. Classify educational data by scale of measurement and state which statistics and claims each scale permits.
  3. Convert raw scores into interpretable derived scores and choose the right test type for a stated decision.

Foundations

Core concepts

Six ideas carry this topic. Each card gives the definition, the wording examiners use, and what the idea looks like in a real test.

6 cards
🧪 01 / 06

Test: a standardised sample of behaviour

A test is a systematic procedure for observing and scoring behaviour under standard conditions. Three features are essential: a representative sample of the behaviour domain, identical conditions for all candidates, and a scoring rule fixed in advance. A test is an instrument — it produces evidence, it does not by itself judge worth.

Exam wording“A test is a systematic procedure for sampling behaviour and scoring it according to a predetermined rule.”

In practiceWhen you write a class test, state the conditions (time, materials, calculators) so that every learner faces the same task.
📏 02 / 06

Measurement: assigning numerals by rule

Measurement assigns numerals to attributes according to explicit rules so that the numbers represent differences in the attribute. Educational attributes are latent, so measurement is indirect: we infer understanding from answers, essays or performances rather than observing it. Hence every score is an estimate with error, which is why reliability and the standard error of measurement matter.

Exam wording“Measurement is the assignment of numerals to objects or events according to rules.”

In practiceWrite the scoring rule down before marking; a mark without a rule is an opinion expressed as a digit.
🔗 03 / 06

Test → measurement → assessment → evaluation

The terms form a chain rather than a list of synonyms. A test collects the sample; measurement quantifies it; assessment interprets it and gives feedback; evaluation judges it against a standard and decides. A test that is never interpreted is measurement without assessment, and marks that never inform a decision are assessment without evaluation.

Exam wording“The test supplies data, measurement quantifies it, assessment interprets it, and evaluation judges it.”

In practiceAfter every class test, write one sentence of interpretation and one decision before you enter the marks.
🪜 04 / 06

Scales of measurement and what they allow

Nominal data are labels with no order (subject choice); ordinal data are ordered but not equally spaced (rank, grade band, Likert response); interval data have equal units but no true zero (most test scores, IQ, temperature); ratio data have equal units and a true zero (time on task, number of items correct out of all items, error counts). The scale decides which statistics are legitimate.

Exam wording“Most educational test scores are treated as interval data, so differences are meaningful but ratios are not.”

In practiceNever say “Ali is twice as able as Bilal” on the basis of test scores — that claim needs a ratio scale with a true zero.
🗂️ 05 / 06

Types of tests and their purposes

Achievement tests sample what has been taught; aptitude tests predict future performance; diagnostic tests locate specific learning difficulties; proficiency tests establish a functional level. Tests also divide by construction (standardized versus teacher-made), by reference (norm-referenced versus criterion-referenced) and by scoring (objective versus subjective). Purpose should select the type, not convenience.

Exam wording“The purpose of the decision determines whether a norm-referenced or a criterion-referenced test is appropriate.”

In practiceAsk “what decision will this result support?” before choosing a test type; if there is no decision, there should be no test.
🔢 06 / 06

Derived scores and honest interpretation

A raw score is uninterpretable on its own. A percentage standardises for total marks; a percentile rank locates a learner within the group; a z-score expresses distance from the mean in standard deviations; a T-score rescales z to a mean of 50 and standard deviation of 10; a stanine divides the distribution into nine bands. Each answers a different question, and each is routinely misread.

Exam wording“A percentile rank of 80 means the learner scored at or above 80 per cent of the group — not that 80 per cent of answers were correct.”

In practiceReport the scale and the reference group alongside any derived score; a percentile without a norm group is meaningless.

04

Application

Worked examples

Three walkthroughs take the definitions to the level the paper asks for: a calculation, a judgement and a repair.

3 walkthroughs
Calculation

From a raw score to a reportable score

A learner scores 38 out of 50 on a class test. The class mean is 32 and the standard deviation is 5. Express the result as a percentage, a z-score and a T-score.

  1. Percentage: divide the raw score by the total and multiply by 100 — 38 ÷ 50 = 0.76, so 76%.
  2. z-score: subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation — (38 − 32) ÷ 5 = 1.2. The learner is 1.2 standard deviations above the class mean.
  3. T-score: rescale the z-score with T = 10z + 50 — 10 × 1.2 + 50 = 62.
  4. Interpretation: 76% describes performance against the paper; z = +1.2 and T = 62 describe position in this class. Neither is a percentile until you consult the distribution.
Result 76% · z = +1.2 · T = 62 — the percentage answers “how much of the paper?”, the z and T answer “where in the group?”
Why this works

Each derived score keeps a different reference. Percentages are criterion-like but depend on the paper’s difficulty; z and T are norm-referenced and depend on the group. Reporting all three without labelling the reference is what makes score reports misleading.

Selection

Choosing the right instrument for the decision

A school must (a) decide which Grade 4 learners need extra support in fractions, (b) certify Grade 10 achievement for college admission, and (c) estimate which Grade 8 learners are likely to cope with an advanced mathematics stream.

  1. For (a), use a diagnostic test built from the fraction sub-skills, interpreted criterion-referenced against each skill so that the specific gap is identified.
  2. For (b), use an achievement test that samples the taught curriculum broadly, with a standardized administration and marking scheme so that results are comparable and defensible.
  3. For (c), use an aptitude or readiness measure, interpreted norm-referenced against a defined group, and treat it as a prediction rather than a verdict.
  4. Check each choice against cost, time and consequences: a diagnostic test is short and low-stakes, while the certification test needs coverage, security and moderation.
Result Diagnostic + criterion-referenced for support; achievement + standardized for certification; aptitude + norm-referenced for prediction.
Why this works

The purpose of the decision, not the convenience of the instrument, should determine the test type and the referencing. Mixing them — for example using a norm-referenced rank to diagnose a specific gap — produces decisions the evidence cannot support.

Judgement

Reading a score report correctly

A parent is told that Ayesha’s score is at the 72nd percentile and that her stanine is 6. The parent concludes that she answered 72 per cent of the questions correctly and is ‘above average but not top’.

  1. Correct the first error: a percentile rank of 72 means she scored at or above 72 per cent of the reference group — it says nothing about the proportion of items correct.
  2. Interpret the stanine of 6: stanines run 1–9 with a mean of 5 and a standard deviation of 2, so 6 is above average but within the middle band, not a top score.
  3. State the missing reference: both scores are meaningless without the norm group — which class, which year, which curriculum.
  4. Ask for the criterion information the parent actually needs: which learning outcomes are secure and which need work, reported separately from rank.
Result Percentile 72 = position in the group, not per cent correct; stanine 6 = above average, middle band; both need a named norm group.
Why this works

Norm-referenced scores answer “where in the group?” while parents usually want “what can my child do?”. A defensible report gives criterion information first and adds rank only as supplementary context.

Practice

Classroom and test-room application

The left column is the professional standard; the right column lists the errors that cost marks and damage results.

do / avoid

✅ Do this

  • State the scoring rule and the test conditions before the test is administered.
  • Name the scale of measurement before choosing a statistic or making a comparison.
  • Report derived scores with their reference group and their meaning in plain words.
  • Match the test type to the decision: diagnostic for gaps, achievement for attainment, aptitude for prediction.
  • Treat every educational score as an estimate with error, and avoid decisions on tiny differences.
  • Keep criterion-referenced information alongside any norm-referenced rank.

🚫 Avoid this

  • Do not describe a test as measuring knowledge directly; it samples behaviour and infers.
  • Do not treat a percentile rank as a percentage of correct answers.
  • Do not calculate a mean for genuinely nominal data or claim ratios from interval scores.
  • Do not report a percentile without naming the comparison group.
  • Do not use an aptitude score as a fixed verdict on a learner’s future.
  • Do not compare raw scores from two tests of different length or difficulty.

Exam traps

Misconceptions

Each myth is a plausible sentence that loses marks. Learn the reality column as the correction you can write.

4 corrections
MythA test measures what a learner knows, directly and exactly.
RealityA test samples behaviour and infers a latent attribute, so every score contains error. Precision depends on the quality of the sample, the conditions and the scoring rule.
MythA percentile rank of 85 means the learner got 85 per cent correct.
RealityIt means the learner scored at or above 85 per cent of the reference group. Percentage of items correct is a different statistic on a different scale.
MythBecause test scores are numbers, they are interval data and ratios are meaningful.
RealityMost test scores are treated as interval at best: the units are equal but there is no true zero, so “twice as able” is not a legitimate claim.
MythStandardized tests are always better than teacher-made tests.
RealityStandardized tests give comparability and technical quality; teacher-made tests give curricular alignment and speed. The better instrument is the one whose purpose matches the decision.

Exam room

Exam-style questions

Answer first, then open the model. The chips show the command word, the mark value and the cognitive level being tested.

2 questions
Explain 6 marks Understand

Explain why educational measurement is described as indirect, and state two consequences of this for interpreting test scores.

Model answer

Educational measurement is indirect because the attributes we care about — understanding, aptitude, attitude — are latent constructs that cannot be observed directly. We observe a sample of behaviour, such as answers to items or a piece of writing, and infer the underlying attribute from it. Two consequences follow. First, every score contains error: the sample may not represent the domain, and the learner’s performance varies with conditions, so scores are estimates rather than exact values, which is why the standard error of measurement and reliability matter. Second, the inference depends on validity evidence: the same score could reflect understanding, memorisation or test-taking skill, so claims about a learner must be limited to what the instrument was designed and shown to measure.

Apply 8 marks Apply

A school has a 40-item test with a mean of 26 and a standard deviation of 4. A learner scores 34. Calculate the z-score and T-score, and explain what each figure tells the school and what it does not.

Model answer

The z-score is (34 − 26) ÷ 4 = 2.0, so the learner is two standard deviations above the class mean — a high position in this group. The T-score is 10z + 50 = 70. Both figures are norm-referenced: they locate the learner relative to this class’s distribution and would change if the group or the test changed. They do not tell the school what the learner can do, which outcomes are secure, or how the learner would perform on a different paper; for those questions the school needs criterion-referenced information at item or outcome level. The scores also carry measurement error, so a difference of a few points between two learners should not be treated as meaningful without reliability evidence.

Compress

Takeaways and revision

Revise these six lines the night before the paper, then tick the checklist once each one is retrievable without notes.

6 lines

Six things to remember

  1. A test samples behaviour under standard conditions and scores it by a rule; it is an instrument, not a judgement.
  2. Measurement assigns numerals by rule, and educational measurement is indirect because the attributes are latent.
  3. The scale of measurement — nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio — limits which statistics and claims are legitimate.
  4. Raw scores become meaningful only as derived scores: percentage, percentile rank, z-score, T-score or stanine.
  5. Reference matters: criterion-referenced scores describe competence, norm-referenced scores describe position.
  6. Purpose selects the test type: diagnostic for gaps, achievement for attainment, aptitude for prediction.
One-line summary

Tests are instruments that sample behaviour, and measurement turns that sample into a number whose meaning depends on the scale used, the reference group and the measurement error attached to it.

💡
Exam tip

If a question says “the learner scored at the 80th percentile”, the marks are for locating the learner in a group — not for percentage correct. If it asks for a z-score, show the formula (X − mean) ÷ SD before substituting; method marks are usually available.
