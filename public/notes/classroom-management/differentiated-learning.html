Differentiated Learning

Same destination, different routes — differentiation is how one teacher manages a class of thirty different learners

⏱️ 9 min read 📚 7 sections 🔑 12 key terms 📝 10 MCQs · 4 CRQs · 1 ERQ

The big idea

Differentiated learning is the deliberate adaptation of content, process, product and environment in response to learner readiness, interests and learning profiles, so that every learner works just beyond their current level with the support they need. It is not individual lesson plans for every child, and it is not lowering standards: it is maintaining a common, high learning goal while varying the route, the support and the demonstration of mastery.

What you will master

  1. Apply Tomlinson’s model: three entry points and four elements to differentiate.
  2. Plan a lesson using pre-assessment, tiered tasks and flexible regrouping.
  3. Distinguish accommodation from modification, and enrichment from more-of-the-same.

Foundations

Core concepts

Each card isolates one idea examiners test. Read the definition first, then the In practice line — that is where scenario questions begin.

🎯 01

Core principles

Differentiation is proactive, goal-driven, flexible and evidence-based. Teachers pre-assess, define a common essential learning goal, then vary tasks, support and products. Tomlinson identifies three learner entry points — readiness, interest and learning profile — and four elements to modify — content, process, product and environment.

In practice

Write one clear objective, then design three routes to it rather than three different objectives.

📊 02

Pre-assessment and formative evidence

Differentiation begins with evidence, not assumption: diagnostic quizzes, hinge questions, exit tickets, prior work and quick conversations reveal readiness and misconceptions, and allow grouping to change as understanding changes.

In practice

Use a five-question hinge question after the explanation and regroup for the practice phase based on the result.

🧱 03

Tiered tasks and scaffolding

Tiered tasks address the same objective at different levels of complexity, structure or abstraction. Scaffolds — worked examples, sentence frames, graphic organisers, partially completed models, glossaries — are temporary and progressively removed.

In practice

Offer the same word problem with a diagram and steps for one group, and without scaffolds for another, both aiming at the same concept.

🔀 04

Flexible grouping and cooperative structures

Groups should be temporary and purpose-driven: readiness groups for targeted teaching, mixed-ability groups for discussion and peer explanation, interest groups for projects. Roles and accountability prevent group work becoming one learner’s work.

In practice

Regroup after each formative check; keep groups fluid and avoid permanent “ability tables”.

🧠 05

Universal Design for Learning and accommodations

UDL designs multiple means of engagement, representation and expression from the outset, benefiting all learners. Accommodations (extra time, larger print, assistive technology, reduced copying) remove barriers without changing the standard; modifications change the standard and should be individual and documented.

In practice

Offer the response in writing, orally or as a labelled diagram where the objective is understanding, not handwriting.

🎁 06

Challenge and enrichment for all

Differentiation includes learners who already exceed the standard. Enrichment should deepen thinking — extension questions, open-ended investigations, teaching a peer, applying to new contexts — rather than simply assigning more of the same work.

In practice

Replace “finish early, do twenty more sums” with “design a problem that uses the same rule in a new context”.

02

Precision

Key vocabulary

Be able to define each term in one sentence. Bold terms below also appear highlighted inside the notes, so you can meet them in context.

Differentiation
Deliberate adaptation of content, process, product and environment in response to readiness, interest and profile.
Readiness
A learner’s current proximity to a specific learning goal — not a measure of general ability.
Interest
The topics, contexts and activities that engage a learner.
Learning profile
Preferences in how a learner accesses and expresses learning; not a fixed “learning style”.
Tiered task
Parallel versions of one task at different levels of complexity or support, aimed at the same objective.
Scaffolding
Temporary support such as worked examples, sentence frames or organisers, progressively removed.
Flexible grouping
Temporary, purpose-driven groups that change as evidence changes.
Universal Design for Learning (UDL)
Designing multiple means of engagement, representation and expression from the outset.
Accommodation
A change that removes a barrier without changing the learning standard.
Modification
A change to the learning standard or expectation itself.
Enrichment
Deeper, open-ended work for learners who already meet the standard.
Pre-assessment
Evidence gathered before teaching to establish readiness and misconceptions.

04

Practice

Classroom application

What to do, and what to avoid, in a real classroom. The left column is the standard to teach; the right column lists the errors that create management problems.

✅ Do this

  • Pre-assess readiness and misconceptions before planning the tasks.
  • Keep one high, shared learning goal and differentiate the route to it.
  • Prepare tiered tasks and scaffolds in advance, not on the spot.
  • Group flexibly and change groups as evidence changes.
  • Offer multiple ways to demonstrate the same learning.
  • Plan genuine enrichment for learners who already meet the standard.

🚫 Avoid this

  • Do not equate differentiation with more worksheets for fast finishers.
  • Do not create permanent ability groups or announce them publicly.
  • Do not lower the standard; adjust support and complexity instead.
  • Do not match instruction to claimed “learning styles” — the evidence is weak.
  • Do not differentiate everything; focus on the lesson’s essential learning.
  • Do not use support as a reward to be withdrawn punitively.
05

Exam traps

Common misconceptions

Each misconception below is a plausible-sounding statement that loses marks. Learn the reality column as a correction you can write.

Myth

“Differentiation means a separate plan for every learner.”

✔ Reality

It means a small number of purposeful routes to a common goal, plus targeted support, which is manageable in a class of thirty.

Myth

“Differentiation lowers standards.”

✔ Reality

Differentiation maintains the standard and changes the support, entry point and demonstration of mastery. Lowering the standard is modification, not differentiation.

Myth

“Fast learners do not need differentiation.”

✔ Reality

Learners who already meet the standard need enrichment that deepens thinking, or they disengage and develop habits of coasting.

Myth

“Matching instruction to learning styles is differentiation.”

✔ Reality

Evidence for sensory learning-style matching is weak. Effective practice offers multiple representations and modalities to everyone rather than diagnosing styles.

Compress

Key takeaways

Revise these lines the night before the paper — they compress the whole note into recall-ready statements.

Six things to remember

  1. Differentiation adapts content, process, product and environment in response to readiness, interest and profile.
  2. It is goal-driven: one high shared objective with multiple routes and support levels.
  3. Pre-assessment and formative evidence make differentiation responsive rather than assumed.
  4. Scaffolds are temporary and should be faded as competence grows.
  5. Flexible, temporary grouping outperforms permanent ability grouping.
  6. UDL designs access for everyone; accommodations remove barriers without changing the standard.
One-line summary

Differentiated learning keeps one high, shared goal while varying content, process, product and environment so that every learner works in their zone of proximal development with the support they need.

💡
Exam tip

Accommodation versus modification is the most common trap. Extra time, larger print or an oral response are accommodations; reducing the number of objectives is a modification.
