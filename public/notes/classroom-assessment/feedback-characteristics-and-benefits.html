Feedback: Characteristics and Benefits

Feedback is not a mark — it is information a learner can act on to close the gap

⏱️ 10 min read 📚 7 sections 🔑 12 key terms 📝 10 MCQs · 4 CRQs · 1 ERQ

The big idea

Feedback is information about the gap between a learner’s current performance and the target performance, together with guidance on how to close it. Hattie and Timperley’s model reduces it to three questions the learner must be able to answer: where am I going, how am I going, and where to next. Feedback differs from praise (which evaluates the person) and from a grade (which summarises). Its power depends less on quantity than on quality: specific, timely, criterion-referenced, actionable feedback improves achievement and self-regulation, while vague or ego-involving comments, or a mark alone, can leave performance unchanged or make it worse.

What you will master

  1. Define feedback and distinguish it from praise and from a grade.
  2. Evaluate any feedback against the criteria of specificity, timing, focus, actionability and learner use.
  3. Design classroom routines — comment-only marking, whole-class feedback, improvement time — that make feedback actually acted on.

Foundations

Core concepts

Each card isolates one idea examiners test. Read the definition first, then the In practice line — that is where scenario questions begin.

🎯 01

What feedback is — and the three questions it must answer

Feedback provides information about the gap between current and target performance. Powerful feedback answers three questions: where am I going (the goal and success criteria), how am I going (the gap), and where to next (the action). If a comment does not help the learner answer the third question, it is description, not feedback.

In practice

Frame comments as: ‘Your goal is X; you have done Y; next do Z.’

🔬 02

Characteristics of effective feedback

Good feedback is specific about what was done and what to change, timely enough to be usable, criterion-referenced rather than compared with peers, task- or process-focused rather than about the person, actionable in a small number of steps, manageable in quantity, and dialogic — the learner must process and respond to it.

In practice

Limit feedback to one strength and one next step that can be achieved in the next piece of work.

🪜 03

Levels of feedback: task, process, self-regulation, self

Feedback operates at four levels: task (correct or incorrect, with correction), process (how to approach the task — strategy, structure, method), self-regulation (how to monitor and check one’s own work), and self (personal praise or criticism). Task and process feedback are the most useful for learning; self-level feedback, especially praise, is the least tied to achievement.

In practice

Move from ‘this is wrong’ up to ‘check your second step — you multiplied before dividing.’

⭐ 04

Feedback versus praise and grades

Praise evaluates the person (‘brilliant work!’) and carries little information. A grade summarises performance and, when given with comments, tends to dominate attention: learners read the number and ignore the guidance. Comment-only feedback therefore produces better learning, with marks added at reporting points rather than on every draft.

In practice

Mark drafts with comments only; reserve numbers for the final, reported version.

🔁 05

Forms of feedback and the loop that closes it

Feedback can be oral or written, immediate or delayed, teacher-, peer- or self-generated, directive (tell the correction) or facilitative (ask a question that prompts discovery). Whatever the form, the loop only closes when the learner uses it — through redrafting, correcting, retrying or reflecting — which is why planned improvement time matters more than more marking.

In practice

Schedule ten minutes of dedicated improvement time after feedback so learners act on it.

📈 06

Benefits: achievement, self-regulation and motivation

Well-designed feedback raises achievement, builds self-regulation and metacognition, reduces the gap between strong and weak learners, makes success criteria visible, and improves the teacher’s own understanding of misconceptions. It also supports motivation when it attributes progress to effort and strategy rather than to fixed ability.

In practice

Track whether the specific next step appears correctly in the following piece of work — that is your evidence of impact.

02

Precision

Key vocabulary

Be able to define each term in one sentence. Bold terms below also appear highlighted inside the notes, so you can meet them in context.

Feedback
Information about the gap between current and target performance, with guidance on how to close it.
Feed-forward
Feedback that focuses on the next task or stage rather than on what has already been completed.
Descriptive feedback
Comment that describes what was done against criteria, without judging the person.
Evaluative feedback
Comment that judges quality or the learner, often without specific guidance.
Praise
Positive judgement directed at the person rather than the work; carries little learning information.
Success criteria
Observable statements describing what a successful performance contains.
Self-regulation
The learner’s ability to monitor, check and direct their own work.
Metacognition
Awareness and management of one’s own thinking and learning strategies.
Peer assessment
Learners giving feedback to each other against shared criteria.
Dialogic feedback
Feedback as an exchange in which the learner responds and the teacher follows up.
Improvement time
Scheduled lesson time in which learners act on feedback and redraft.
Feedback loop
The complete cycle of evidence, comment, learner action and re-checking.

04

Practice

Classroom application

What to do, and what to avoid, in a real classroom. The left column is the standard to teach; the right column lists the errors that cost marks and learning.

✅ Do this

  • State the success criteria before the task so feedback refers to something shared.
  • Give one strength and one next step rather than a page of corrections.
  • Focus comments on the task and the process, not on the learner’s character.
  • Return feedback while learners can still use it on the same kind of work.
  • Plan improvement time so learners rewrite, redraft or retry.
  • Use peer and self-assessment against criteria to multiply feedback without multiplying your marking.

🚫 Avoid this

  • Do not write only a grade and expect the comment beside it to be read.
  • Do not give vague encouragement such as ‘good effort’ and call it feedback.
  • Do not correct every error; select the errors that matter for the current objective.
  • Do not deliver feedback in public when it concerns a learner’s standing or ability.
  • Do not give feedback so late that the class has moved to another topic.
  • Do not treat feedback as one-directional — check that the learner has acted on it.
05

Exam traps

Common misconceptions

Each misconception below is a plausible-sounding statement that loses marks. Learn the reality column as a correction you can write.

Myth

“More marking means better feedback.”

✔ Reality

Quantity is not quality. A page of corrections overloads the learner; two specific, actionable comments used in improvement time change performance more than extensive marking.

Myth

“Praise is feedback and raises achievement.”

✔ Reality

Praise evaluates the person and carries little task information. Task and process comments, which describe the work against criteria, have far stronger effects.

Myth

“Giving marks with comments is the most efficient feedback.”

✔ Reality

Marks tend to dominate attention; learners look at the number and ignore guidance. Comment-only feedback on drafts, with marks at reporting, protects the learning value.

Myth

“Feedback has been given once the comment is written.”

✔ Reality

Feedback exists only when the learner acts on it. Without response time and a re-check, a comment is a record of intent, not feedback.

Compress

Key takeaways

Revise these lines the night before the paper — they compress the whole note into recall-ready statements.

Six things to remember

  1. Feedback closes the gap between current and target performance; a grade only reports the gap.
  2. It must answer: where am I going, how am I going, and where to next.
  3. Specificity, timing, criterion reference, actionability and dialogue are the core characteristics.
  4. Task and process feedback beats praise and personal comments.
  5. The loop closes only when learners act on feedback in improvement time.
  6. Well-designed feedback builds self-regulation, motivation and achievement at the same time.
One-line summary

Feedback is information about the gap between current and target performance that tells the learner what to do next; it works when it is specific, timely, criterion-referenced, actionable and acted upon.

💡
Exam tip

If a scenario describes comments such as ‘good job’, ‘neat work’ or ‘must try harder’, the answer is almost always evaluative feedback or praise, not descriptive feedback. Look for the comment that names a criterion and a next step.
