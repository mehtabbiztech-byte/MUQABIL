Developing Assessment-Embedded Lesson Plans

Plan backwards from the evidence — if you cannot say what you will look for, the objective is not yet a plan

⏱️ 10 min read 📚 7 sections 🔑 12 key terms 📝 10 MCQs · 4 CRQs · 1 ERQ

The big idea

An assessment-embedded lesson plan is a plan in which assessment is designed at the same time as the activities, not added afterwards. It starts from constructive alignment: the learning outcome, the teaching activity and the evidence of learning must all point at the same thing. Backward design makes this concrete — identify the desired result, decide what would count as acceptable evidence, and only then plan the learning experiences. In practice this means every plan contains stated outcomes, shared success criteria, a diagnostic opening, at least one mid-lesson check for understanding with a decision rule, and an end-of-lesson exit ticket whose results shape the next lesson.

What you will master

  1. Write a lesson outcome and success criteria that make the evidence of learning observable.
  2. Apply backward design and constructive alignment to sequence a lesson from outcome to evidence to activity.
  3. Build a lesson assessment map with diagnostic, hinge and exit points, each with an explicit decision rule.

Foundations

Core concepts

Each card isolates one idea examiners test. Read the definition first, then the In practice line — that is where scenario questions begin.

🔗 01

Constructive alignment: outcome, activity, evidence

Constructive alignment (Biggs) requires that the intended learning outcome, the activity learners do, and the assessment task all address the same verb at the same cognitive level. If the outcome says ‘compare’, an activity of copying definitions and a test item asking for recall are misaligned, and no amount of marking will fix the design.

In practice

Underline the verb in your outcome, then check that the activity and the assessment both require that verb.

⏪ 02

Backward design: evidence before activity

Backward design (Wiggins and McTighe) proceeds in three stages: identify the desired results, determine what would count as acceptable evidence, then plan learning experiences. Planning activities first usually produces busy lessons whose learning cannot be demonstrated. Planning evidence first forces clarity about what ‘success’ looks like before the lesson begins.

In practice

Draft the exit ticket before you draft the group activity.

🎯 03

Outcomes and success criteria that can be seen

A usable outcome names an observable performance: ‘Learners will be able to convert between fractions, decimals and percentages for common values.’ Success criteria unpack it into what the learner’s work will contain — for example, ‘I can convert 3/4 to 0.75 and to 75% and explain the method.’ Criteria must be visible to learners, not kept in the plan.

In practice

Write criteria in the first person (‘I can…’) and display them for the whole lesson.

🧭 04

The opening diagnostic: start where they are

Every lesson should begin with a two- to five-minute check of the prerequisite knowledge the new learning depends on. This is diagnostic assessment at lesson scale: a quick problem, a sorting task, or three recall questions. Its purpose is to decide whether to proceed, briefly repair, or change the planned entry point.

In practice

Identify the one prerequisite idea the lesson cannot work without, and test only that.

⏱️ 05

Mid-lesson checks and decision rules

A check for understanding — a hinge question, mini-whiteboards, cold call, or a quick pair explanation — sits at the point where the lesson would otherwise move from teaching to practice. It is only useful if the plan states a decision rule in advance: for example, ‘if more than a third answer B, reteach with a diagram before practice.’

In practice

Write the decision rule next to the question on the plan so you act in the moment rather than improvise.

🎟️ 06

The exit ticket and the next-lesson handover

The exit ticket is a short end-of-lesson task that produces evidence from every learner in under five minutes. Its design determines the next lesson: the plan should state how the tickets will be sorted and what each group will do next. Assessment-embedded planning therefore joins two lessons — the evidence collected at the end of one becomes the opening activity of the next.

In practice

Sort exit tickets into three piles — secure, partial, not yet — and start the next lesson with the middle pile.

02

Precision

Key vocabulary

Be able to define each term in one sentence. Bold terms below also appear highlighted inside the notes, so you can meet them in context.

Constructive alignment
Designing outcomes, activities and assessment so that all three require the same learning at the same cognitive level.
Backward design
Planning from desired results to acceptable evidence to learning experiences.
Learning outcome
An observable statement of what learners will know or be able to do by the end of a lesson or unit.
Success criteria
Learner-facing statements describing what a successful performance contains.
Check for understanding
A quick mid-lesson assessment used to decide whether to proceed, repair or reteach.
Hinge question
A single diagnostic question at a decision point in the lesson, with distractors for common misconceptions.
Decision rule
A pre-planned response specifying what the teacher will do for each likely pattern of results.
Exit ticket
A short end-of-lesson task that captures every learner’s understanding.
Diagnostic assessment
Assessment of prerequisite knowledge and misconceptions before new teaching.
Scaffolding
Temporary support that is withdrawn as competence develops.
Formative task
A task whose primary purpose is to generate evidence and feedback during learning.
Table of specifications
A blueprint that maps assessment items to content and cognitive levels.

Practice

Classroom application

What to do, and what to avoid, in a real classroom. The left column is the standard to teach; the right column lists the errors that cost marks and learning.

✅ Do this

  • Write the learning outcome as an observable performance with one clear verb.
  • Derive success criteria from the outcome and display them to learners.
  • Begin with a short diagnostic check of the prerequisite idea.
  • Plan one check for understanding with a written decision rule.
  • Design the exit ticket before designing the activities.
  • Plan how the evidence will change the next lesson, and note it on the plan.

🚫 Avoid this

  • Do not write activities first and invent an objective to fit them.
  • Do not use outcomes such as ‘understand’ or ‘know’ with no observable evidence.
  • Do not plan an assessment you have no intention of acting on.
  • Do not put the only check at the end of the lesson; by then it is summative for that lesson.
  • Do not let the exit ticket become a graded test — keep it low-stakes and fast.
  • Do not ignore the results; an unread exit ticket is paperwork.
05

Exam traps

Common misconceptions

Each misconception below is a plausible-sounding statement that loses marks. Learn the reality column as a correction you can write.

Myth

“A lesson plan only needs objectives and activities; assessment belongs to the end of the unit.”

✔ Reality

If assessment is only at the end, teaching cannot respond. Assessment-embedded plans place evidence points inside the lesson so that instruction can change while it still matters.

Myth

“Adding assessment to a lesson means adding a test.”

✔ Reality

Most embedded assessment is questioning, observation, whiteboards and a two-item exit ticket. It adds minutes, not papers.

Myth

“Backward design is only for whole units, not single lessons.”

✔ Reality

The same logic scales down: decide the evidence, then the activities. Drafting the exit ticket first is backward design in miniature.

Myth

“A well-written objective guarantees that the lesson is aligned.”

✔ Reality

Constructive alignment also requires the activity and the assessment to demand the same cognitive level as the objective. Many plans have correct objectives and misaligned tasks.

06

Compress

Key takeaways

Revise these lines the night before the paper — they compress the whole note into recall-ready statements.

Six things to remember

  1. Constructive alignment ties outcome, activity and evidence to the same verb and cognitive level.
  2. Backward design plans evidence before activities.
  3. Success criteria must be observable and visible to learners.
  4. Every lesson needs an opening diagnostic, a mid-lesson check with a decision rule, and an exit ticket.
  5. The exit ticket is the bridge to the next lesson, not the end of assessment.
  6. Assessment-embedded planning adds minutes of design time and saves hours of unfocused marking.
One-line summary

An assessment-embedded lesson plan is built backwards from observable outcomes and success criteria, with a diagnostic opening, a mid-lesson check tied to a decision rule, and an exit ticket that shapes the next lesson.

💡
Exam tip

If a question asks how to make a lesson plan assessment-embedded, answer with the sequence — outcome and success criteria, diagnostic, hinge question with a decision rule, exit ticket, next-lesson action — and name constructive alignment once.
