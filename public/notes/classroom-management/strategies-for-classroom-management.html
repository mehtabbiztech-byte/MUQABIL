🏫 Classroom Management

Strategies for Classroom Management

A tiered toolkit: prevent most disruption, correct calmly and early, and support the few who need more

💡 Core idea: Classroom management strategies work best as a tiered system rather than a list of tricks. Tier one is proactive: relationships, expectations, routines, engaging instruction and physical design prevent most disruption. Tier two is corrective and low-key: proximity, signals, private reminders and choices stop emerging behaviour before it escalates. Tier three is intensive and individual: functional assessment, behaviour plans and partnership with families and specialists. The skill is choosing the least intrusive strategy that will actually work.

🎯 Core Concepts

🛡️
Tier 1 — Proactive strategies
Prevention includes co-constructed expectations, taught routines, high but realistic expectations, strong relationships, engaging and appropriately challenging instruction, purposeful movement around the room, and positive narration of what learners are doing well.
In practiceSpend the first two weeks teaching routines and reinforcing them; the investment returns all year.
👀
Kounin’s group-management techniques
Withitness (noticing before it spreads), overlapping (managing two things at once), smoothness (no jerky transitions), momentum (no dead time), group alerting (keeping everyone accountable) and format variety reduce disruption without correction.
In practiceKeep the whole class accountable by asking a question, pausing, then calling on a learner at random rather than only on volunteers.
🤫
Low-key corrective strategies
The least intrusive options include proximity, eye contact, a gesture, a name plus brief instruction, planned ignoring of low-level attention-seeking paired with praise for the desired behaviour, and private reminders. These preserve dignity and avoid escalation.
In practiceHold up two fingers and point to the rule on the wall instead of announcing a correction across the room.
⚖️
Consequences and behaviourist tools
Logical consequences should be related, respectful, reasonable and reliably applied. Positive reinforcement — specific praise, recognition, token systems, group contingencies — builds the behaviour you want. Behaviour contracts and self-monitoring suit individual cases.
In practicePraise the process (“you waited and then asked”) rather than the person (“good boy”) so the behaviour is repeatable.
🗣️
Communication and de-escalation
Calm voice, neutral body language, I-statements (“I need the talking to stop so the group can hear”), limited choices (“you can work here or at the front table”), and time to comply reduce power struggles. Ginott’s congruent communication avoids sarcasm and labelling.
In practiceGive a choice plus a short period to comply; then follow through calmly rather than repeating the demand louder.
🧩
Tier 3 — Intensive individual support
When behaviour persists, use ABC data, a functional behaviour assessment, an individual behaviour plan with replacement behaviours, self-monitoring, mentor check-ins, and collaboration with families, counsellors and specialists. Restorative repair follows incidents.
In practiceWrite a plan that teaches a replacement behaviour (“ask for a break card”) rather than only listing sanctions.

🏫 Classroom Application

✅ Do this

  • Choose the least intrusive strategy likely to work, then follow through calmly.
  • Narrate the positive: describe what learners are doing correctly three to four times as often as you correct.
  • Use proximity and private reminders before public correction.
  • Make consequences related, respectful, reasonable and consistent.
  • Offer limited choices rather than ultimatums.
  • Use ABC data and a replacement behaviour for persistent cases.

🚫 Avoid this

  • Do not escalate in public; it invites a power struggle you cannot win.
  • Do not use sarcasm, humiliation or collective punishment.
  • Do not repeat a demand louder without follow-through.
  • Do not remove break or lunch time as a routine sanction.
  • Do not confiscate support (scaffolds, movement, sensory tools) as a punishment.
  • Do not write behaviour plans that list only sanctions and no replacement behaviour.

⚠️ Common Misconceptions

❌
“You should never smile before Christmas.”
Warmth and structure are complementary. Coldness does not create respect; predictable expectations plus genuine care do.
❌
“Consequences must be severe to be effective.”
Consistency matters far more than severity. Severe consequences increase resentment and avoidance while teaching nothing.
❌
“Praise will spoil learners.”
Specific, process-focused praise increases the behaviour it names and supports self-efficacy. Vague or controlling praise is what becomes hollow.
❌
“A behaviour plan is a list of punishments.”
A functional behaviour plan identifies the function of behaviour and teaches a replacement that meets the same need, alongside environmental adjustments and consequences.

🔑 Key Takeaways

Remember these points for the exam:
  • Most disruption is prevented by proactive Tier 1 practice, not corrected after the fact.
  • Kounin’s techniques — withitness, overlapping, smoothness, momentum, group alerting — remain the practical core.
  • Use the least intrusive correction that will work; escalate only when lower tiers fail.
  • Consequences should be related, respectful, reasonable and reliably applied.
  • Praise and reinforce the behaviour you want far more than you punish the behaviour you don’t.
  • Persistent behaviour needs functional assessment and a replacement behaviour, not more sanctions.
📌 One-line summary: Effective management is tiered: prevent through relationships, routines and engaging teaching; correct early and quietly; and support persistent behaviour with functional assessment and a taught replacement.
